## Models already learn routing from drivers' executed plans, but no published diffusion model has yet been trained on real multi-stop logistics plans

The premise of this report is that a generative model trained on a firm's record of good logistics decisions would absorb what "good" looks like and produce new plans in the same spirit. The premise has real support: a public benchmark was built on it, inverse optimisers and sequence models have been trained on thousands of real driver routes, and diffusion models learn well from human demonstrations in robotics and from logged human driving. What this research could not find, in any venue, repository or dataset registry searched up to September 2026, is published work in which a diffusion model is trained on real executed delivery routes or dispatch plans. The only case known to this report is its own unpublished prototype, described below.

### The Amazon challenge scored imitation, not efficiency

The 2021 Amazon Last Mile Routing Research Challenge, run with MIT's Center for Transportation and Logistics, asked entrants to learn "from historical routes executed by Amazon delivery drivers" ([registry entry](https://github.com/awslabs/open-data-registry/blob/main/datasets/amazon-last-mile-challenges.yaml); [dataset paper, Merchán et al.](https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.2022.1173)). The registry states 9,184 routes in total, 6,112 for training and 3,072 for evaluation. Counting the files in the [public S3 bucket](https://amazon-last-mile-challenges.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2) confirms 6,112 training routes from 17 stations driven in July and August 2018, with 60 to 1,133 routes per station, but only 3,052 evaluation routes, twenty fewer than stated. Of the training routes, 2,718 are rated High, 3,292 Medium and 102 Low.

The target was the sequence the driver followed. The official score is the sequence deviation multiplied by the edit distance with real penalty on normalised travel times and divided by the number of edits that distance prescribes, score = SD × ERP_norm / ERP_e, so a perfect copy of the driver scores zero ([MIT-CAVE scoring README](https://github.com/MIT-CAVE/rc-cli/blob/main/scoring/README.md); [scoring code](https://github.com/MIT-CAVE/rc-cli/blob/main/scoring/score.py)). The benchmark rewards imitation fidelity, not cost or punctuality. "Success" enters only through `route_score`, which "is based both on the level of time window adherence and the amount of backtracking in the observed sequence" ([rc-cli data structures](https://github.com/MIT-CAVE/rc-cli/blob/main/templates/data_structures.md)).

That label is confounded. In this report's count of the training files, High routes average 140.6 stops against 154.0 for Medium ones, and the High share ranges from 32.9 percent at stations coded BO to 50.2 percent at those coded SE, so the rating partly reflects workload and geography. Top entrants nonetheless used it: the winners' code weights High routes twice and Medium routes one and a half times when matching zone patterns ([Cook, Held and Helsgaun code](https://github.com/heldstephan/jpt-amz/blob/main/scripts/analyze_zone_id_order.py)), and the AWS implementation of Wu et al. drops Low routes ([preprocessing code](https://github.com/aws-samples/amazon-sagemaker-amazon-routing-challenge-sol/blob/main/preprocessing.py)).

### What the leading approaches learned

The winners, William Cook, Stephan Held and Keld Helsgaun ([*Transportation Science*](https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.2022.1185)), mined each station's zone visiting order and ran a modified LKH whose penalty counts re-entries into zones and their parent groupings and can also charge time-window lateness ([penalty code](https://github.com/heldstephan/jpt-amz/blob/main/LKH-AMZ/SRC/Penalty.c)). The third-placed team, Sky is the Limit, learned no statistical model of sequences: it scaled travel times by zone-code similarity and solved with a genetic algorithm ([team code](https://github.com/rasitabay/lastmile_challenge/blob/main/src/model_apply.py)). Published leaderboard scores could not be opened and are not reported here.

| Entry | How history enters | Sequencing engine | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cook, Held and Helsgaun | Zone order mined per station, High routes weighted double | LKH with zone-entry and lateness penalties | First place |
| Sky is the Limit | Hand-set penalties on zone-code dissimilarity | Genetic algorithm | Third place |
| Wu, Song, March and Duthie | Prediction by Partial Matching over zone sequences, Low routes dropped | Rollout, then OR-Tools or LKH within zones | 0.0374 with LKH (self-reported) |
| Zattoni Scroccaro et al. | Inverse optimisation of arc costs, per depot | TSP solver | 2nd of 48 finalists (self-reported) |
| LKH (near-optimal) tour, this report | None | LKH on released travel times | 0.0776 on 1,000 training routes |

Wu et al.'s [repository](https://github.com/aws-samples/amazon-sagemaker-amazon-routing-challenge-sol) trains on 6,000 route sequences in seconds on one CPU (self-reported). Zattoni Scroccaro, van Beek, Mohajerin Esfahani and Atasoy learn arc costs by inverse optimisation, one model per depot ([*Transportation Science*](https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.2023.0241); [code](https://github.com/pedroszattoni/amazon-challenge)). Özarık, da Costa and Florio's "pool and select" ranks candidate sequences with a learned score ([*Transportation Science*](https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.2022.0029); [code](https://github.com/amflorio/amzn-routing-challenge)), the closest existing generate-and-rank design. The common lesson is that the valuable knowledge in drivers' routes was coarse and legible, namely which neighbourhood follows which, and a classical solver did the rest.

### Why drivers deviate, and whether it pays

Mo, Wang, Guo, Winkenbach and Zhao write that drivers "frequently deviate from planned delivery routes because of their tacit knowledge of the road and curbside infrastructure, customer availability, and other characteristics of the respective service areas" ([*Transportation Research Part E*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tre.2023.103168); [abstract mirror](https://github.com/alexfanjn/arXiv-daily-graphMining/blob/master/info/23-Jan/11/paper_info.json)), and Graph2Route (KDD 2022) notes "significant deviations" between couriers' actual and planned routes ([abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2022/kdd/Graph2Route%3A%20A%20Dynamic%20Spatial-Temporal%20Graph%20Neural%20Network%20for%20Pick-up%20and%20Delivery%20Route%20Prediction)). The primary measurement of local drivers against routing services is Ceikute and Jensen, "Routing Service Quality: Local Driver Behavior Versus Routing Services" (IEEE MDM 2013, pages 97 to 106, DOI 10.1109/MDM.2013.20; [bibliographic record](https://github.com/SJTU-YONGFU-RESEARCH-GRP/CIRDC/blob/1ab56d1088cea9ece4fa03cd1844d3e8425a86f0/CIRDC/6568397/2013.json)). Its full text could not be opened; a later paper summarises it as finding "substantial differences between popular routes and recommended routes" ([CrowdPlanner full-text mirror](https://github.com/drywaters/cs157a-project/blob/77f259714d1d4ab55f3fffc105576ed506e5f0e2/big_data/6075.txt), secondary), and the authors' 2015 follow-up states that "local drivers often prefer routes that are neither shortest nor fastest" ([MDM 2015 record](https://github.com/NicolasV15/CIRDC-Pro/blob/d9ac67fa108032e41467f8fa26c7c221ae184e44/articleInfo/Conferences/1000468/7263115.json)). These studies concern single origin to destination trips, not stop sequences.

Amazon's data contain no planned routes, so this report compared each driver's sequence with an LKH (near-optimal) tour minimising the released travel times, on a seeded random sample of 1,000 training routes ([analysis script](03_driver_vs_lkh_analysis.py); [summary](03_driver_vs_lkh_summary.txt)). The LKH tours are heuristic, run five times on travel times rounded to tenths of a second, with no proof of optimality. Drivers' tours took 14.9 percent more travel time on average (median 13.8 percent). Of 9,897 stops with time windows, drivers arrived late at 1.7 percent and the LKH tours at 5.9 percent, although LKH was not given the windows, and drivers re-entered a zone 0.8 times per route against 13.1 for LKH. The lateness estimate lets an early vehicle proceed without waiting for a window to open, treats the earliest window end among a stop's packages as its deadline, and uses planned service times and average travel times. The LKH tours score 0.0776 on the challenge metric. High and Medium routes barely differ on these measures. On this evidence executed routes trade travel time for punctuality and tidy zone coverage, which is what a generator trained on them would absorb; no study was found that measures real cost or customer outcomes of driver-imitating plans against optimised ones.

### Inverse optimisation, preference learning and imitation

Inverse optimisation recovers the cost an observed decision-maker appears to optimise. It began with Burton and Toint's inverse shortest path problem (*Mathematical Programming* 53, 1992; [secondary bibliography record](https://github.com/tecnarayan/cs768-2025-assignment/blob/main/dataset_papers/2206.01484v1/main.bbl), primary record unreachable) and was formalised by [Ahuja and Orlin (*Operations Research* 49(5), 2001)](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.49.5.771.10607). Ziebart, Maas, Bagnell and Dey's maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning (AAAI 2008) learned from taxi drivers' route choices ([secondary summary](https://github.com/gofhir/neural-network-knowledge/blob/b8ccab27e623553c84158673b5fc8ed0cb474ee4/site/content/papers/maxent-irl-ziebart-2008.md), primary record unreachable), and Google Maps scaled the idea to "hundreds of millions of states and demonstration trajectories", reporting a 16 to 24 percent gain in route quality ([ICLR 2024 abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2024/iclr/Massively%20Scalable%20Inverse%20Reinforcement%20Learning%20in%20Google%20Maps), self-reported). For multi-stop routing the direct ancestor is Canoy and Guns, who estimate stop-to-stop transition probabilities from past executed solutions to steer a solver ([CP 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-30048-7_4)), later with a neural network ([CP 2021](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.CP.2021.42)).

Plain behavioural cloning predicts the expert's next step, and Ross, Gordon and Bagnell showed that because the learner's future inputs depend on its own choices this "leads to poor performance in theory and often in practice" ([AISTATS 2011 record](https://github.com/mlresearch/v15/blob/gh-pages/_posts/2011-06-14-ross11a.md)). A stop-by-stop route imitator drifts in exactly this way, which is one argument for generating a whole plan at once, as diffusion does.

### Diffusion learning from demonstrations and from logged driving

Diffuser plans "by iteratively denoising trajectories" ([ICML 2022](https://github.com/mlresearch/v162/blob/gh-pages/_posts/2022-06-28-janner22a.md)), and Decision Diffuser models a policy "as a return-conditional diffusion model", adding constraints and skills as conditions ([ICLR 2023 abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2023/iclr/Is%20Conditional%20Generative%20Modeling%20all%20you%20need%20for%20Decision%20Making%3F)). Diffusion Policy by Chi and colleagues is the strongest robotics precedent: the RSS 2023 paper benchmarks 12 tasks from 4 benchmarks ([RSS 2023 abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2023/rss/Diffusion%20Policy%3A%20Visuomotor%20Policy%20Learning%20via%20Action%20Diffusion)), the journal version extends this to 15 tasks ([extended paper, full-text mirror](https://github.com/liuskywalkerjskd/robotics-papers/blob/main/2303.04137/paper_text.txt)), and both report an average improvement of 46.9 percent (self-reported). It predicts a chunk of future actions, executes part and re-plans, a receding horizon that mirrors rolling re-optimisation of remaining stops, and its authors concede it inherits cloning's weakness when demonstrations are inadequate.

Closer to executed vehicle plans are models trained on logged human driving. MotionDiffuser (Jiang and colleagues at Waymo, CVPR 2023) learns the joint distribution of future trajectories of multiple agents and reports state-of-the-art results on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset ([CVPR abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2023/cvpr/MotionDiffuser%3A%20Controllable%20Multi-Agent%20Motion%20Prediction%20Using%20Diffusion); [author list](https://github.com/aras62/vision-based-prediction/blob/4fd23fe54238b6fa3c62f80f3d9f78d39414f66e/papers/trajectory/2023.md)). Its constrained sampling "based on differentiable cost functions" is the template for imposing hard business rules at generation time. DiffusionDrive (Liao and colleagues, CVPR 2025) truncates the diffusion schedule to two steps and reports 88.1 PDMS on the NAVSIM planning benchmark at 45 frames per second ([CVPR abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2025/cvpr/DiffusionDrive%3A%20Truncated%20Diffusion%20Model%20for%20End-to-End%20Autonomous%20Driving); [code](https://github.com/hustvl/DiffusionDrive), self-reported). This evidence shows that diffusion can absorb large volumes of real, multimodal human vehicle behaviour, but it carries over only partly: a driving plan is a few seconds of continuous motion, while a delivery plan is a permutation of a given stop set under customer time windows and capacity, where one misplaced stop breaks feasibility.

Trajectory generators such as DiffTraj (NeurIPS 2023), which synthesises GPS trips as "privacy-free alternatives" ([abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2023/neurips/DiffTraj%3A%20Generating%20GPS%20Trajectory%20with%20Diffusion%20Probabilistic%20Model); [code](https://github.com/Yasoz/DiffTraj)), and ControlTraj (KDD 2024, [abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2024/kdd/ControlTraj%3A%20Controllable%20Trajectory%20Generation%20with%20Topology-Constrained%20Diffusion%20Model)), learn real single trips with no customers, stop sequence or success label. Diffusion for routing itself, such as DIFUSCO, trains on tours produced by Concorde and LKH-3 rather than on executed plans ([NeurIPS 2023 paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/0ba520d93c3df592c83a611961314c98-Paper-Conference.pdf); [data README](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/data/README.md)).

This project's own prototype ([diffusion_lastmile](../../diffusion_lastmile/README.md)), an unpublished experiment by this report, is to our knowledge the only case of diffusion trained on real executed delivery routes. It trains a DIFUSCO-style stop-level model and a zone-level model only on the 2,718 High routes. On 300 routes from the evaluation set, the zone-level model followed by OR-Tools scored 0.0409 against 0.0523 for a zone-change penalty and 0.0535 for historical zone frequencies alone, with 3.23 hours of travel against 3.31 for drivers and 3.00 for plain OR-Tools; the stop-level model scored 0.0763 and learned mostly local structure ([results summary](../../diffusion_lastmile/results/summary.md)). These are single-run results on a subset, not comparable to challenge leaderboard scores.

### Label quality: what counts as a success

A business history contains good, ordinary and poor plans, and diffusion offers several ways to use the grading beyond simply discarding weak examples. Classifier-free guidance jointly trains conditional and unconditional models and blends them at sampling time ([Ho and Salimans, abstract record](https://github.com/allenai/genesys/blob/main/model_discovery/model/library/tree/classfreediffu.json)), so a model trained on all routes with `route_score` as a condition could be pushed towards "High". Reward-weighted training scales each example's likelihood by a quality score, as Lee and colleagues did with human-feedback rewards for text-to-image models ([abstract mirror](https://github.com/TTXS123OK/CVPapers/blob/f9e468972cafc06e54554b4efb392cca463741ce/cs.CV/2023/02/20230223.md)). Diffusion-DPO (Wallace and colleagues, CVPR 2024) optimises directly on pairwise preferences, fine-tuning Stable Diffusion XL on 851,000 crowdsourced comparisons ([CVPR abstract](https://github.com/swkim101/cspapers.org/blob/92397bd6e2418b494f37b1c102c6706050b70480/index2/2024/cvpr/Diffusion%20Model%20Alignment%20Using%20Direct%20Preference%20Optimization)); a dispatcher choosing between two plans supplies exactly such pairs. None of these has been tested on routing in any work found, and each inherits the confounding above: a label that tracks easy days teaches the model to recognise easy days.

### Conditioning, data volume, rights and privacy

No paper found conditions a routing generator on industry or company; the evidence concerns depots and time. Zone codes are local labels, so station identity, date, capacity, stop count and time-window density must be conditions. Shift is visible within Amazon's release, whose evaluation routes extend into June, September and October 2018. No study measures how imitation quality scales with routes per depot; robotics offers an anchor of 200 proficient demonstrations per robomimic task ([documentation](https://github.com/ARISE-Initiative/robomimic/blob/master/docs/datasets/robomimic_v0.1.md)), while Amazon's busiest station contributed 1,133 routes.

Data rights settle the commercial question. The Amazon material is released under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 licence ([registry entry](https://github.com/awslabs/open-data-registry/blob/main/datasets/amazon-last-mile-challenges.yaml)), so it supports research but not a commercial model. A business would have to train on its own executed routes, which ties the idea's viability to how many well-labelled routes that business holds per depot. Its own data raise privacy duties: the GDPR counts "location data" among identifiers of personal data ([Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 4(1)](http://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex:32016R0679), snippet), which covers customers' addresses and each driver's workday. Four spatio-temporal points identified 95 percent of people in a mobile-phone dataset ([de Montjoye et al., *Scientific Reports* 2013](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep01376), snippet), and Carlini and colleagues found that diffusion models memorise training data and warned that "synthetic data does not give privacy for free" ([USENIX Security 2023, full-text mirror](https://github.com/anlp-team/LTI_Neural_Navigator/blob/main/final_data/Daphne%20Ippolito_Extracting%20Training%20Data%20from%20Diffusion%20Models.txt)).

### Evidence gaps

The official leaderboard, technical proceedings and most publisher pages could not be read, so challenge scores other than self-reported ones are not given. The full texts of Ceikute and Jensen (2013), Ziebart et al. (2008) and Burton and Toint (1992) could not be opened, and their findings rest on secondary records. Published planned-versus-actual deviation rates for delivery fleets were not found. The driver-versus-LKH comparison uses planned service times, average travel times, no waiting at windows and a heuristic tour rather than Amazon's own plan. The prototype results are unpublished and unreplicated. The absence of diffusion trained on real logistics plans refers to public work to September 2026 and cannot exclude unpublished industrial systems.
