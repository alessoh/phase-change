## A diffusion model trained on good plans learns where good plans lie, not what is allowed

A diffusion model trained on successful logistics plans learns an approximate probability distribution over good plans, conditioned on the problem each plan solved. Sampling from that distribution proposes a new plan that resembles past successes, and that is the precise sense in which such a model "knows what to expect". It does not guarantee that a proposal is feasible, that it is optimal, or that it is sensible when tomorrow looks unlike the training data, and every competitive published system examined here pairs the model with search, repair or an explicit objective.

### Destroying structure and learning to restore it

The intuition is simple. Take a good plan, blur it a little at a time until nothing of it remains, and train a network to undo one small blur at a time. Once trained, the network can start from pure noise and restore, step by step, something that looks like a good plan, even though it has never seen that particular one.

The idea of slowly destroying structure and learning to restore it ([Sohl-Dickstein et al., ICML 2015](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/sohl-dickstein15.html), snippet) was made practical by Ho, Jain and Abbeel as the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model, or DDPM ([abstract, reproduced in the diffusers docs](https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/main/docs/source/en/api/pipelines/ddpm.md)). The forward, blurring process involves no learning. The released code uses $T = 1000$ steps whose noise variances $\beta_t$ rise linearly from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$ ([DDPM run script](https://github.com/hojonathanho/diffusion/blob/master/scripts/run_cifar.py)), and any noise level can be reached in one jump: with $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}(1-\beta_s)$, the noised example is $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$ for standard Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ ([DDPM utilities](https://github.com/hojonathanho/diffusion/blob/master/diffusion_tf/diffusion_utils_2.py)). A network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ is trained by squared error to recover the added noise, which the authors connect to "denoising score matching with Langevin dynamics" (abstract above). Generation runs the chain backwards, each step blending the current state with the network's estimate of the clean example and then re-injecting a little fresh noise ([DDPM utilities](https://github.com/hojonathanho/diffusion/blob/master/diffusion_tf/diffusion_utils_2.py)). That re-injected noise is why repeated runs return different plans, which is what makes drawing several samples and keeping the cheapest worthwhile.

### The continuous view: scores, SDEs and ODEs

Song and colleagues recast noising as a stochastic differential equation that can be run backwards if one knows, at every noise level, the score $\nabla_x \log p_t(x)$: the direction in which data becomes more probable ([score SDE, ICLR 2021](https://github.com/yang-song/score_sde)). The reverse equation $dx = [f(x,t) - g(t)^2\nabla_x \log p_t(x)]\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar w$ retraces the forward drift while climbing the score, and a deterministic "probability flow" ODE shares the same marginals (same source). A trained noise predictor already is a score estimator, $\nabla_x \log p_t(x) \approx -\epsilon_\theta(x,t)/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$, which is how the official code computes scores ([score_sde_pytorch](https://github.com/yang-song/score_sde_pytorch/blob/main/models/utils.py)).

### Making sampling fast

Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models keep DDPM training but define a non-Markovian reverse process that samples "10× to 50× faster in terms of wall-clock time" ([DDIM abstract, diffusers docs](https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/main/docs/source/en/api/pipelines/ddim.md), self-reported); setting its noise parameter $\eta$ to zero gives the fully deterministic sampler ([diffusers DDIM scheduler](https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/main/src/diffusers/schedulers/scheduling_ddim.py)).

Flow matching, introduced by Lipman and colleagues as "a simulation-free approach for training CNFs based on regressing vector fields of fixed conditional probability paths" ([abstract, reproduced by a journal club](https://github.com/CarlaSa/diffusion_journal_club_24)), learns a velocity rather than noise. In the straight-line case, with $x_0$ noise and $x_1$ data, the training point is $x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$ and the target velocity is $x_1-x_0$ ([TorchCFM code](https://github.com/atong01/conditional-flow-matching/blob/main/torchcfm/conditional_flow_matching.py)). Straighter paths need fewer solver steps, and rectified flow's "reflow" straightens them further toward one-step generation ([Rectified Flow](https://github.com/gnobitab/RectifiedFlow), self-reported).

Consistency models learn a function that maps every point on a probability flow trajectory to the same clean endpoint, so one evaluation from pure noise yields a sample. Song and colleagues report a one-step CIFAR-10 FID of 3.55 by distillation and note that the models can also be trained standalone ([abstract, diffusers docs](https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/main/docs/source/en/api/pipelines/consistency_models.md), self-reported); improved consistency training later reached one-step FID 2.51 without a teacher ([ECT README table](https://github.com/locuslab/ect), self-reported).

### Discrete diffusion for routes and graphs

A route is a set of yes or no decisions about which stop follows which, and Gaussian noise fits such data awkwardly. D3PM instead corrupts categorical variables with transition matrices ([Austin et al., NeurIPS 2021](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/file/958c530554f78bcd8e97125b70e6973d-Paper.pdf), snippet). Its code implements a uniform family that drifts toward any state with equal probability, an absorbing family that sends values to a mask state, and a discretised Gaussian family that prefers nearby ordinal values ([D3PM image code](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/master/d3pm/images/diffusion_categorical.py)), plus a nearest-neighbour family for text built from embedding neighbours ([D3PM text code](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/master/d3pm/text/diffusion.py)). DIFUSCO, the reference diffusion solver for routing, uses the two-state uniform case: one bit per edge, per-step matrix $Q_t=(1-\beta_t)I+\tfrac{\beta_t}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$, so after $t$ steps an edge keeps its original value with probability $(1+\bar\alpha_t)/2$ ([DIFUSCO schedulers](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/difusco/utils/diffusion_schedulers.py)). Its released configurations use this Bernoulli noise for the travelling salesman problem but Gaussian noise for one independent-set benchmark ([reproducing scripts](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/reproducing_scripts.md)), so the advantage of discrete noise is task dependent.

A binary matrix has no ordinary gradient of log density, so the network predicts, for each edge, the probability that it belongs to the clean solution, and the sampler draws the next, less noisy matrix from the implied posterior. The discrete counterpart of the score is a set of probability ratios between neighbouring states, which score-entropy methods estimate directly ([SEDD, "Estimating the Ratios of the Data Distribution"](https://github.com/furiosa-ai/ParallelBench/blob/main/packages/Score-Entropy-Discrete-Diffusion/README.md)). In language modelling, masked (absorbing) diffusion now leads this family: MDLM reports state-of-the-art perplexity among diffusion models and an absorbing D3PM implementation that beats SEDD on LM1B ([MDLM README](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/mdlm), self-reported). Discrete flow matching brings the flow view to categorical data ([Campbell et al. code](https://github.com/andrew-cr/discrete_flow_models); [Meta flow_matching library](https://github.com/facebookresearch/flow_matching)).

The problem instance enters as graph features. DIFUSCO's anisotropic graph network embeds stop coordinates as node features and the noisy edge states as edge features, adding a timestep embedding in every layer ([gnn_encoder.py](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/difusco/models/gnn_encoder.py)). At logistics scale the matrix is not dense. For 500 or more cities DIFUSCO keeps only each city's $k$ nearest neighbours, with $k$ of 50 at 500 cities and 100 at 1,000 and 10,000 ([reproducing scripts](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/reproducing_scripts.md); [dataset code](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/difusco/co_datasets/tsp_graph_dataset.py)), so a 10,000-city instance carries one million candidate edges rather than one hundred million, by our arithmetic. Demands, time windows and capacities would enter the same way, as further node, edge or global features, and ML4CO-Bench-101 lists its generative consistency solver (contributed from Fast T2T) and its generative COExpander solver as supporting capacitated vehicle routing ([ML4CO-Bench-101 README](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/ML4CO-Bench-101)).

### Conditioning and guidance

The network learns $p(x\mid c)$ for today's instance $c$. Classifier guidance, from Dhariwal and Nichol, steered sampling by adding the gradient of a separately trained classifier to the diffusion score ([guided-diffusion](https://github.com/openai/guided-diffusion)). Ho and Salimans removed the classifier: "we jointly train a conditional and an unconditional diffusion model, and we combine the resulting conditional and unconditional score estimates" ([abstract, paper text mirror](https://github.com/minhphuc477/KLTN/blob/main/PAPERS/by_topic/diffusion_latent/ho2022cfg.md)). The diffusers library computes $\hat\epsilon=\epsilon_u+w(\epsilon_c-\epsilon_u)$ ([pipeline code](https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/main/src/diffusers/pipelines/stable_diffusion/pipeline_stable_diffusion.py)); larger $w$ trades diversity for fidelity.

Energy or reward guidance replaces the label with a business cost $E(x)$. The target becomes a tilted distribution proportional to $p(x\mid c)\,e^{-\lambda E(x)}$, whose score is $\nabla_x\log p(x\mid c)-\lambda\nabla_x E(x)$; this identity is exact for clean data and only approximate at intermediate noise levels. Discrete edges have no gradient, so T2T differentiates through the relaxed clean heatmap instead. Its code estimates tour cost as $\sum_{ij} d_{ij}\,p_\theta(x_{0,ij}=1\mid x_t)$, back-propagates to the noisy edge variables, and multiplies each edge's posterior by $\exp(-\text{gradient})$ before resampling ([T2T model](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/T2TCO/blob/main/diffusion/pl_tsp_model.py); [posterior step](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/T2TCO/blob/main/diffusion/pl_meta_model.py)), which we read as a first-order form of the exponential tilt. T2T reports "an average performance gain of 49.15% for TSP solving" over the previous state of the art ([T2T README](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/T2TCO), self-reported). Fast T2T applies the same guidance to a consistency model ([Fast T2T code](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/Fast-T2T/blob/main/diffusion/consistency/tsp.py)). Guidance can also enforce constraints: Projected Diffusion Models recast each sampling step as a constrained optimisation that keeps samples inside a specified region ([PDM README, NeurIPS 2024](https://github.com/RAISELab-atUVA/Projected-Diffusion)), with guarantees reported only for convex sets ([arXiv 2402.03559](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.03559), snippet), which routing constraints are not.

### How many steps deployed solvers actually use

The thousand-step chain is a training device, not a deployment cost. DIFUSCO trains on 1,000 steps ([train.py](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/difusco/train.py)) but samples in 50 on a cosine skip schedule ([reproducing scripts](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/reproducing_scripts.md)), and Fast T2T's scripts use one to five steps plus a few guided rewrites ([Fast T2T README](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/Fast-T2T); [TSP-500 script](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/Fast-T2T/blob/main/scripts/eval_tsp500.sh)).

| Family | What the network learns | Sampling steps in practice |
|---|---|---|
| DDPM, image setting | Added noise, equivalently the score | 1,000 |
| DDIM | Same network as DDPM | Tens (10 to 50 times faster, self-reported) |
| Discrete diffusion for routing (DIFUSCO, T2T) | Per-edge probability of the clean tour | 50, from a 1,000-step training schedule |
| Flow matching, rectified flow | Velocity along a chosen path | Few when paths are straight |
| Consistency (iCT; Fast T2T for routing) | Direct map to the trajectory endpoint | One to five |

### What "knowing what to expect" means

Stated exactly, the model approximates $p(\text{plan}\mid\text{instance})$, and sampling draws a plan that is plausible under it. The case for a distribution rather than a single answer is multimodality: an instance can have several distinct near-optimal edge sets of almost equal cost, differing in which neighbourhood is visited first or where two loops are joined. A pointwise predictor is pulled toward the average of such alternatives, a blur of half-used edges, while a sampler commits to one. T2T's authors describe exactly this aim, to "estimate the high-quality solution distribution for each instance" ([Fast T2T README](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/Fast-T2T)). Direction adds a further, artificial mode only because DIFUSCO trains on a directed matrix in which a tour and its reverse differ; its decoder removes it by symmetrising.

The evidence that this mechanism is what delivers quality is mixed. In ML4CO-Bench-101's summary table the best learned result on 50 and 100-city problems comes from a non-generative one-shot supervised heatmap with search ([Table 8](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/ML4CO-Bench-101/blob/main/assets/performance.png), self-reported), as it does on clustered 50 and 100-city sets ([Table 9](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/ML4CO-Bench-101/blob/main/assets/performance_cross.png), self-reported), while the generative COExpander leads at 500 cities with a 0.257 percent gap to Concorde (Table 8). Xia and colleagues report that with finely tuned search even an all-zero heatmap on 500 cities surpassed UTSP, an unsupervised non-diffusion method, and that heatmap-guided search trails LKH-3 ([ICML 2024 repository](https://github.com/xyfffff/rethink_mcts_for_tsp)). Much of the quality therefore comes from search, and none of the newer discrete methods above has a routing result we could find.

For routing with vehicles the picture is starker. On capacitated vehicle routing the benchmark's best learned entries are constructive, not generative, and trail the HGS solver by 1.18 to 2.36 percent on synthetic sets and 5.47 percent on the real-world CVRPLIB collection, though they run faster ([Table 8](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/ML4CO-Bench-101/blob/main/assets/performance.png), self-reported).

The premise of this report also goes beyond the published training data. DIFUSCO's 50 and 100-city data come from an earlier public dataset ([data README](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/data/README.md)), and its 128,000, 64,000 and 6,400 training instances at 500, 1,000 and 10,000 cities are random instances labelled by default with the LKH-3 heuristic at 1,000 trials ([generate_tsp_data.py](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/data/generate_tsp_data.py)). The files are named "concorde", but the labels are strong heuristic tours, not certified optima. No published system learns from a firm's own history, and an archive of dispatchers' plans would carry their mistakes as faithfully as their skill. Data volume matters too: Kadkhodaie and colleagues found image denoisers memorise small sets and generalise once trained on roughly $10^5$ examples of 80 by 80 pixel images ([ICLR 2024 repository](https://github.com/LabForComputationalVision/memorization_generalization_in_diffusion_models), self-reported). That threshold belongs to images; no study measures it for routing plans.

### A worked intuition: four stops

Take a depot 0 and customers 1, 2 and 3 on the route 0, 1, 2, 3, 0. DIFUSCO's dense target is the directed 4 by 4 matrix with a one at entry $(i,j)$ when stop $j$ follows stop $i$ ([dataset code](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/difusco/co_datasets/tsp_graph_dataset.py)): 16 bits, four of them ones, at (0,1), (1,2), (2,3) and (3,0). The reversed route is its transpose. Under the training schedule each bit keeps its value with probability about 0.83 at step 200 and 0.54 at step 500, nearly a coin toss (our calculation). Given the coordinates, the network outputs a probability for each bit, and the decoder adds the matrix to its transpose before building a tour ([tsp_utils.py](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/difusco/utils/tsp_utils.py)). Suppose the symmetrised scores are 0.92 for pair (0,1), 0.88 for (1,2), 0.83 for (0,2), 0.81 for (2,3), 0.64 for (0,3) and 0.10 for (1,3); these numbers are invented for exposition. The greedy decoder ranks pairs by score divided by length and skips any that would close a loop early. With equal lengths it accepts (0,1) and (1,2), rejects (0,2) because it would strand stop 3, accepts (2,3) and closes 3 to 0. For several vehicles the depot row would hold more ones, and capacity cannot be read off the matrix at all, since it depends on which customers share a route.

### What this does not guarantee

Feasibility is not guaranteed, because the model learns what is probable rather than what is permitted; capacity, time windows and driver hours hold only if decoding, projection or a downstream solver enforces them. Optimality is not guaranteed either, which is why DIFUSCO's scripts draw four samples and keep the best ([reproducing scripts](https://github.com/Edward-Sun/DIFUSCO/blob/main/reproducing_scripts.md)). Out-of-distribution behaviour is the third gap: COExpander's 0.257 percent on uniform 500-city instances widens to 0.774 percent on Gaussian and 1.998 percent on clustered ones ([Table 9](https://github.com/Thinklab-SJTU/ML4CO-Bench-101/blob/main/assets/performance_cross.png), self-reported). A new depot or demand spike is such a shift, so a diffusion model is best treated as a learned proposal generator paired with constraint checking and an explicit objective.

### Evidence gaps

Paper-level claims rest on official code, READMEs, documentation that reproduces abstracts, or search snippets, because the primary paper pages could not be opened; the Lipman and Ho and Salimans abstracts come from third-party mirrors. We did not confirm that COExpander is strictly a diffusion model, rather than a generative model of another kind, nor the per-method CVRP scores of the benchmark's generative solvers, since Table 8 shows only the best entry. The four-stop heatmap is invented, and no study we found measures how many historical plans a firm needs.
