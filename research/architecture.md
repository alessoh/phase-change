Chapters 1 through 5 establish the physics, 6 through 11 the methods, 12 through 17 the domain applications, and 18 through 20 the critique and the frontier. Each chapter is built only on results the dossiers verified, and where verification issued a correction I have used the corrected form.

**1. What a Phase Transition Is, and How We Learned That Mean Field Lies**

The book opens by refusing the loose usage of the word transition and building the strict one. It begins with Gibbs, whose memoir on heterogeneous equilibrium defines chemical potential, the phase rule, and the interfacial free energy that is the thermodynamic ancestor of every nucleation barrier, then turns to Ehrenfest's 1933 proposal to classify transitions by the lowest singular derivative of the thermodynamic potential, noting through Tilman Sauer's translation and commentary that the first-order category survived while the higher-order ones did not, because real continuous transitions diverge as power laws rather than jumping. Landau then supplies the vocabulary the rest of the book will use, the order parameter, the free-energy expansion, the symmetry-subgroup rule, and the mean-field exponents. The chapter's argument arc turns on the collapse of those exponents. Lenz proposed the model, Ising solved it in one dimension and found no transition, wrongly extrapolating upward, and Peierls's contour argument showed that two dimensions does order. Onsager's exact solution produced a logarithmically divergent specific heat and a definite critical temperature, Yang later derived the spontaneous magnetization and the exponent one eighth, and Guggenheim had already shown that eight different fluids collapse onto one coexistence curve with an exponent near one third rather than the van der Waals one half. Built on Gibbs 1878, Ehrenfest 1933 with Sauer's 2017 commentary, Landau 1937, Ising 1925, Peierls 1936, Onsager 1944, Yang 1952, and Guggenheim 1945. The reader leaves asking why substances with nothing microscopically in common should share an exponent at all.

**2. Universality and the Renormalization Group**

This chapter answers the question chapter one leaves open, and it is the conceptual engine for everything the book later says about machines that coarse-grain. The argument moves from phenomenology to mechanism. Widom's scaling hypothesis posits that the singular free energy is a generalized homogeneous function, from which the exponent relations follow as equalities, including the relation Rushbrooke had already established two years earlier as an inequality. Kadanoff's block-spin construction gives that hypothesis a physical rationale by replacing cells of spins with effective spins and arguing the coarse-grained system is again Ising with rescaled couplings. Wilson turns coarse-graining into a differential flow in coupling space, where critical points are fixed points and exponents are eigenvalues of the linearized flow, so that distinct microscopic Hamiltonians flowing to the same fixed point are forced to share exponents. Wilson and Fisher then make the flow calculable by expanding about four dimensions, and Fisher's review states precisely what a universality class depends on, namely dimensionality, order-parameter components, and interaction range. The chapter closes on credit and emergence, noting that the 1982 Nobel went to Wilson alone though the 1980 Wolf Prize was shared with Fisher and Kadanoff, and reading Anderson's argument that reductionism does not imply constructionism with broken symmetry as its central case. Built on Widom 1965, Rushbrooke 1963, Kadanoff 1966, Wilson 1971, Wilson and Fisher 1972, Fisher 1974, the 1982 Nobel citation, and Anderson 1972. The reader leaves wondering what happens to this machinery when there is no local order parameter to flow, and why three-dimensional Ising exponents still come from bootstrap and Monte Carlo rather than from an exact solution.

**3. Transitions Landau Cannot Name**

Here the book breaks its own framework on purpose, and this is the chapter that later justifies why machine learning was invited into the field at all. Mermin and Wagner prove that continuous symmetries cannot break at finite temperature in two dimensions with short-range interactions, which appears to forbid ordering in the two-dimensional XY model. Berezinskii, publishing first, and then Kosterlitz and Thouless, identify the escape, an order that is topological rather than conventional, with vortex and antivortex pairs bound below the transition and free above it, a transition of infinite order in the Ehrenfest sense whose correlation length diverges exponentially, and Kosterlitz supplies the renormalization-group treatment the following year. The 2016 Nobel, split one half to Thouless and one quarter each to Haldane and Kosterlitz, formalizes the recognition while Berezinskii, who died in 1980, could not share it. The chapter then drops the temperature axis entirely. Pfeuty's exact solution of the transverse-field Ising chain gives the canonical quantum critical point, Hertz builds a quantum Landau-Ginzburg-Wilson functional with imaginary time, a dynamical exponent, and an effective dimension, later extended by Millis, and Sachdev's monograph describes the fan of anomalous behaviour that a zero-temperature critical point throws upward into finite temperature. Built on Mermin and Wagner 1966, Berezinskii 1970, Kosterlitz and Thouless 1973, the 2016 Nobel citation, Pfeuty 1970, Hertz 1976 with the Millis extension, and Sachdev's Quantum Phase Transitions. The reader leaves asking whether the order-parameter-only framework can survive contact with metals, where strange-metal resistivity and local quantum criticality appear to defy it.

**4. Disorder, Frustration, and the Rugged Landscape**

This chapter introduces the mathematics that will return, unexpectedly, as the theory of learning in chapter twenty, so it is placed in foundations rather than in the frontier. It opens with the purely geometric case, Broadbent and Hammersley's percolation, a continuous transition with critical exponents and universality classes but no Hamiltonian and no temperature. It then turns to systems where the interactions themselves are random. Edwards and Anderson propose that oscillating RKKY couplings leave no net order yet freeze spins into fixed random directions below a temperature, and define the order parameter that explains the observed susceptibility cusp. Sherrington and Kirkpatrick make the problem solvable by coupling every pair, and the replica-symmetric solution fails visibly, producing negative entropy at zero temperature. Parisi's resolution replaces a single order parameter with a function on the unit interval obtained by breaking replica symmetry hierarchically, describing a landscape of many pure states organized ultrametrically, a solution later proved rigorously by Talagrand building on Guerra's interpolation bound and recognized by half of the 2021 Nobel, the other half going jointly to Manabe and Hasselmann for climate modelling. Built on Broadbent and Hammersley 1957, Edwards and Anderson 1975, Sherrington and Kirkpatrick 1975, Parisi 1979 and 1980, Guerra and Talagrand, and the 2021 Nobel citation. The reader leaves with a genuinely unsettled question, whether the short-range three-dimensional spin glass shows Parisi's full replica symmetry breaking or the two-state droplet picture of Fisher and Huse, since the proof covers only the infinite-range model.

**5. Kinetics: Nucleation, Metastability, and the Spinodal**

Thermodynamics says which phase is stable, and says nothing about whether a system will get there, so this chapter separates the two questions and it is the physical foundation for the simulation chapters that follow. Volmer and Weber set the competition between a favourable bulk term and an unfavourable interfacial term, producing a barrier at a critical nucleus size and a rate exponential in that barrier. Becker and Döring recast nucleation as a birth-and-death process in cluster-size space and derive the steady-state rate with the Zeldovich correction. Turnbull and John Crocker Fisher adapt the theory to condensed systems by adding an interfacial transport term, the form used for crystallization from melts, and the basis for Turnbull's demonstration that liquid metals supercool to roughly four fifths of their melting temperature. Ostwald's rule of stages then introduces a purely kinetic principle, that a system tends to form first the least stable phase nearest in free energy and to transform stepwise, which is why polymorph selection is not a thermodynamic question. Cahn and Hilliard replace the sharp interface with a square-gradient functional, the ancestor of all phase-field modelling, and Cahn shows that inside the spinodal there is no barrier and no critical nucleus at all, only the amplification of long-wavelength fluctuations at a fastest-growing wavelength that sets the microstructural scale. Built on Volmer and Weber 1926, Becker and Döring 1935, Turnbull and Fisher 1949, Ostwald 1897, Cahn and Hilliard 1958 with Part III of 1959, and Cahn 1961. The reader leaves asking whether the crisp dichotomy between nucleation and spinodal decomposition survives at short interaction range, and whether classical nucleation theory, which routinely misses absolute rates by many orders of magnitude, should be read as a law or an idealization.

**6. Learning to Recognize a Phase**

The methods half of the book begins with the simplest and most consequential move, treating a Monte Carlo configuration as an image and asking a classifier to name the phase. Carrasquilla and Melko trained an ordinary feed-forward network on raw two-dimensional Ising configurations, recovered the critical temperature without being given a Hamiltonian or an order parameter, and then pushed a convolutional variant into the Ising gauge theory, where no local order parameter exists. Van Nieuwenburg, Liu, and Huber removed the need for labels by deliberately mislabelling data at a proposed transition point and reading the true point off the central peak of a W-shaped accuracy curve, demonstrated on the Kitaev chain, the classical Ising transition, and a many-body-localization transition. The chapter then follows the difficulty upward. Ch'ng, Carrasquilla, Melko, and Khatami trained a three-dimensional convolutional network on determinant quantum Monte Carlo configurations and recovered the antiferromagnetic phase diagram of the cubic Hubbard model at half filling, and Broecker, Carrasquilla, Melko, and Trebst showed that a network fed the sampled Green's function, not the auxiliary field, locates transitions even where a severe sign problem makes conventional correlators useless. Schindler, Regnault, and Neupert located the many-body-localization transition from entanglement spectra of individual eigenstates, and Venderley, Khemani, and Kim decoded two distinct localized phases plus a thermal one with a sharper boundary than entanglement entropy gives. Tomoki and Tomi Ohtsuki applied convolutional networks to eigenfunction images of disordered two-dimensional electron systems, judging phase from a single eigenfunction while explicitly deferring the critical exponent to later work. The reader leaves with the question that organizes chapter eight, namely what feature a correct classifier is actually using.

**7. Discovery Without Labels**

If supervised classification requires knowing the answer, unsupervised methods promise to find phases nobody named, and this chapter tests that promise carefully rather than celebrating it. Lei Wang showed that plain principal component analysis of raw Ising configurations extracts a leading component proportional to the magnetization, with further components carrying structure-factor information, and Wetzel systematically compared principal component analysis, manifold learning, clustering, and variational autoencoders on the two-dimensional Ising and three-dimensional XY models, finding that latent variables track the known order parameters and that reconstruction loss alone flags a transition. Hu, Singh, and Scalettar supplied the necessary corrective by asking which transitions and crossovers these methods cannot resolve, across square and triangular Ising, Blume-Capel, a highly degenerate biquadratic spin-one model, and the XY model. Ce Wang and Hui Zhai then showed the approach survives genuine multiplicity, tracking two order parameters and two transitions in the frustrated XY model on triangular and union-jack lattices. The chapter's most interesting results concern order that principal components cannot see. Rodriguez-Nieva and Scheurer used diffusion maps to cluster XY configurations by winding number and capture the Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless transition without feature engineering, Liu and van Nieuwenburg's discriminative cooperative networks generalized the confusion scheme to fully unlabelled data, Kottmann, Huembeli, Lewenstein, and Acín reframed discovery as anomaly detection and mapped the extended Bose-Hubbard phase diagram, and Scheurer and Slager clustered Hamiltonians by topological equivalence class through learned adiabatic deformation paths. The reader leaves asking whether any of these methods has ever found a phase that was not already known to the person running them.

**8. Making the Classifier Testify**

This chapter is the methodological conscience of the book, and it argues that a correct classification is not yet a physical result. Wetzel and Scherzer reconstructed a trained network's decision function as an explicit function of its inputs when that function is sufficiently symmetric, reading out the magnetization for the Ising model and the Polyakov loop for SU(2) lattice gauge theory. Ponte and Melko showed that support vector machines can learn the explicit mathematical form of order parameters and Hamiltonian constraints for the ferromagnetic Ising model, a conserved-order-parameter variant, and the Ising gauge theory. Greitemann, Liu, and Pollet built tensorial-kernel support vector machines whose decision function is directly an explicit multipolar order parameter, detecting hidden nematic order, and with Jaubert, Yan, and Shannon extended the method to frustrated magnets, where it recovers not only hidden order parameters but the emergent ice-rule constraints that define a classical spin liquid. Zhang and Kim's quantum loop topography sits on the other side of the same argument, since it succeeds precisely because a human encoded the relevant response function into loop observables, later extended with Melko to spinon and vison mutual statistics. The chapter's two sharpest results are sceptical. Beach, Golubeva, and Melko showed that for the XY model a single-layer network fails, a convolutional network succeeds by learning global magnetization, and only a purpose-designed deep network learns vortices without feature engineering. Arnold and Schäfer derived the optimal analytical predictors that several detection schemes converge to at infinite capacity, so that the network can be replaced outright by a closed-form expression. The reader leaves asking how much of the field's apparent discovery is rediscovery of what the descriptor set already contained.

**9. Wave Functions Made of Weights**

The book now moves from classifying states to representing them, and the argument is a comparison rather than an advertisement. Carleo and Troyer parameterized the amplitude of a many-body wave function with a restricted Boltzmann machine and optimized it by stochastic reconfiguration for the transverse-field Ising and Heisenberg models, and Deng, Li, and Das Sarma showed that such states can carry volume-law entanglement with polynomially many parameters while short-range-connected ones obey an area law, establishing that the ansatz is not bounded the way matrix product states are. The chapter then measures the claim against the incumbent, White's density matrix renormalization group and its matrix-product-state reformulation by Schollwöck, which remain the accuracy baseline. Progress came from architecture and optimization rather than from the original ansatz, with Choo, Neupert, and Carleo reaching competitive energies on the frustrated square-lattice J1-J2 model, Roth, Szabó, and MacDonald showing that deep group-convolutional states outperform prior methods on frustrated magnets, and Chen and Heyl removing the parameter-count bottleneck with minimum-step stochastic reconfiguration and producing evidence for a gapless spin liquid. The chapter's two honest results are Czischek, Gärttner, and Gasenzer's finding that convergence degrades badly for quenches near the Ising critical point, exactly where the physics is interesting, and the thirty-three-author V-score benchmark led by Wu, Rossi, Vicentini, and Carleo, which defines a dimensionless accuracy metric from energy and variance and exposes which Hamiltonians remain unsolved by every method. The reader leaves asking whether the observed advantage over tensor networks in two dimensions is general or confined to models where bond dimension happens to bind.

**10. Potentials That Can Reach the Transition**

First-order transitions demand quantum accuracy in energy differences of a few millielectronvolts per atom and simultaneously demand hundreds of thousands of atoms for hundreds of nanoseconds, and this chapter is about the dissolution of that tradeoff. Behler and Parrinello decomposed the total energy into atomic contributions predicted from symmetry-invariant descriptors, Bartók, Payne, Kondor, and Csányi recast the same problem as Gaussian process regression, Zhang, Han, Wang, Car, and E made the approach linear-scaling and symmetry-preserving, and the equivariant generation, NequIP from Batzner and Kozinsky and colleagues and MACE from Batatia and Csányi and colleagues, cut the data requirement by orders of magnitude, culminating in the general-purpose MACE-MP-0 trained on the roughly 1.6 million structures of the Materials Project Trajectory dataset. The payoff is specific and checkable. Zhang, Wang, Car, and E computed a full water phase diagram from a SCAN-trained model, correct for every stable polymorph except ices III and XV. Piaggi, Weis, Panagiotopoulos, Debenedetti, and Car computed homogeneous ice nucleation rates by seeding across a broad range of supercoolings at ab initio accuracy. Niu, Bonati, Piaggi, and Parrinello reproduced the gallium phase diagram and showed that metastable beta-gallium is kinetically preferred over the stable alpha phase, a direct experimental instance of Ostwald's rule from chapter five. Cheng, Mazzola, Pickard, and Ceriotti found the molecular-to-atomic crossover in dense liquid hydrogen to be supercritical rather than first order, and Sosso, Behler, and Bernasconi, later Abou El Kheir and colleagues, resolved crystallization kinetics in phase-change memory alloys. The reader leaves asking about the quantity that actually sets the barrier, the solid-liquid interfacial free energy and its anisotropy, which no benchmark yet certifies.

**11. Rare Events and the Coordinate Problem**

A potential that can reach the transition still cannot watch it happen, because the barrier makes the crossing vanishingly rare, and this chapter is about the two families of remedies and the machine-learning turn that unified them. The biasing family begins with Laio and Parrinello's metadynamics, in which a history-dependent bias fills the free-energy wells along chosen collective variables, is stabilized by Barducci, Bussi, and Parrinello's well-tempered variant, and is recast by Invernizzi and Parrinello as the direct construction of a target probability distribution, which degrades far more gracefully when the variables are poor. The trajectory family begins with Dellago, Bolhuis, Csajka, and Chandler's transition path sampling, which needs no prior guess at the mechanism and defines the reaction coordinate afterward through the committor. Machine learning entered precisely at the choice of variable. Ma and Dinner first automated reaction-coordinate identification by fitting the committor, Mardt, Pasquali, Wu, and Noé collapsed the entire Markov-state-model pipeline into a single network trained on a variational score, and the Parrinello group's family of learned variables, Deep-LDA from data confined to basins, Deep-TICA from already-biased trajectories reweighted to equilibrium, and Deep-TDA mapping onto a preassigned target distribution, made learned coordinates deployable. Noé, Olsson, Köhler, and Wu sidestepped dynamics altogether with Boltzmann generators that sample equilibrium configurations in one shot. Most recently Kang, Trizio, and Parrinello computed the committor self-consistently from endpoint information alone, closing the loop back to 1998. The reader leaves with the sharpest methodological question in the book, whether the slowest mode and the reactive coordinate are the same object when the transition is strongly first order.

**12. Predicting and Generating Matter**

The applications half opens with the domain that has drawn both the largest claims and the largest corrections, and this chapter reports the construction while chapter eighteen tests it. The bottom layer is the high-throughput database, the Materials Project led by Jain and Persson, the Open Quantum Materials Database from Saal, Kirklin, and Wolverton, and AFLOW from Curtarolo, resting on the grand-potential convex-hull construction Ong, Wang, Kang, and Ceder established for the lithium-iron-phosphate-oxygen system. Kirklin's benchmark sets the honest noise floor, since the mean absolute error between calculated and measured formation energies, 0.096 electronvolts per atom, is barely above the scatter between independent measurements, 0.082. Alongside sits the older CALPHAD tradition that Kaufman and Ågren call the true birth of the materials genome, and the structure-search baselines any learned method must beat, Pickard and Needs's random searching and Oganov and Glass's evolutionary algorithm. The middle layer is surrogate modelling, Xie and Grossman's crystal graph convolutional network and the universal potentials M3GNet and CHGNet. The top layer is generative, Xie's crystal diffusion variational autoencoder and its descendant MatterGen from Zeni and colleagues, and the screening campaign GNoME from Merchant and colleagues, reporting 2.2 million structures below the hull of which 381,000 were called newly stable. Bartel and colleagues supply the warning that governs the chapter, that accurate formation energies do not imply accurate stability classification, and Riebesell's Matbench Discovery formalizes the misalignment between regression metrics and the classification that discovery actually requires. The reader leaves asking what a zero-temperature hull distance can possibly say about a compound that in reality is compositionally disordered.

**13. Diffraction and the Self-Driving Beamline**

This chapter follows the same problem into the laboratory, where the question is no longer which compound to make but which measurement to take next. Phase identification came first, with Park, Sohn, and colleagues classifying crystal system, extinction group, and space group directly from simulated powder patterns treated as images, Oviedo, Buonassisi, and colleagues solving the small-data problem with physics-informed augmentation and classifying real thin-film halide samples, and Szymanski, Bartel, Zeng, Tu, and Ceder adding a probabilistic ensemble and a branching search that untangles multi-phase mixtures. Maffettone, Olds, and colleagues then built the crystallography companion agent to return calibrated probabilities rather than confident answers, so that it works beside a beamline scientist rather than instead of one, and Banko, Maffettone, Naujoks, Olds, and Ludwig turned representation learning on combinatorial datasets into novelty detection, which is the practical signature of an undiscovered phase region. The second thread replaces the human's choice of measurement. Noack, Yager, Fukuto, Doerk, Li, and Sethian introduced Gaussian-process-steered acquisition at NSLS-II, later extended for inhomogeneous noise and anisotropic kernels and generalized across facilities in the cross-facility review with Zwart, Rotenberg, and colleagues, while Kusne's CAMEO mapped a composition-phase diagram on the fly at a synchrotron and located an optimized phase-change material. Doerk and colleagues then found previously unknown coexisting and hybrid block-copolymer morphologies that a grid scan would have stepped over, and the A-Lab from Szymanski, Zeng, Ceder, and colleagues closed the loop into robotic synthesis. The reader leaves asking whether an acquisition policy that minimizes model uncertainty is the same thing as a policy that finds an unknown boundary.

**14. Microscopes and Scattering Instruments That Decide**

Where chapter thirteen watches ensembles, this chapter watches individual atoms and individual scattering events, and its argument is that the closed loop changes the epistemic status of the instrument. Ziatdinov, Dyck, Kalinin, and colleagues turned atomically resolved scanning transmission electron microscopy into a stream of machine-readable labels, identifying atoms by species and tracking silicon dopants switching coordination in graphene, and Maksov and colleagues converted an in situ movie of electron-beam-induced transformation in tungsten disulfide into quantitative statistics over defect populations, which is a phase transformation rendered as data. Roccapriore, Dyck, Oxley, Ziatdinov, and Kalinin then put deep kernel learning inside a live four-dimensional experiment so the microscope chose its own probe positions. The same group moved the loop into scanning probe microscopy, first with Liu, Kelley, and colleagues discovering during the experiment which ferroelectric domain motifs give the largest hysteresis loops, then with hypothesis learning, in which the instrument maintains competing physical models of bias-induced switching and selects the measurements that discriminate between them. The scattering side contributes two results of a different kind, Samarakoon, Batista, Tennant, and colleagues inverting time-of-flight neutron measurements of the spin ice dysprosium titanate into an effective spin Hamiltonian with confidence intervals, and Chitturi, Turner, and colleagues training a neural implicit representation of the dynamical structure factor and differentiating through it to recover exchange parameters directly from inelastic scattering data. The reader leaves with the problem the chapter cannot solve, that an electron beam both observes and induces the transformation, and no accepted framework separates the transition from the probe that drove it.

**15. Programmable Quantum Matter as Ground Truth**

This chapter inverts the direction of authority, because for the first time the experiment can prepare states the theory cannot compute. Greiner, Mandel, Esslinger, Hänsch, and Bloch's observation of the superfluid-to-Mott-insulator transition in an optical lattice founded the field, and Mazurenko and colleagues in Greiner's group realized a Fermi-Hubbard antiferromagnet whose correlation length reached the system size, which is precisely the benchmark the variational methods of chapter nine are asked to reproduce. Keesling and colleagues in Lukin's group used a programmable Rydberg simulator to test the quantum Kibble-Zurek mechanism, measuring the universal scaling of correlation length with ramp rate and observing corrections beyond the prediction, Ebadi and colleagues mapped checkerboard, striated, and star phases across a 256-atom array, and Semeghini and colleagues placed 219 atoms on kagome links and detected a toric-code-type spin liquid through topological string operators. Google Quantum AI's analogue-digital processor then revealed a coarsening-induced breakdown of Kibble-Zurek scaling in the XY model alongside Kosterlitz-Thouless signatures. Machine learning enters as the reading instrument. Rem, Käming, Sengstock, Weitenberg, and colleagues extracted the Haldane topological phase diagram and characterized the superfluid-to-Mott transition from single-shot momentum images, Bohrdt, Grusdt, Knap, and colleagues used a classifier to adjudicate quantum gas microscope snapshots between a doped resonating-valence-bond liquid and geometric string theory, favouring the latter to intermediate doping, and Miles, Kim, and colleagues identified boundaries in Rydberg snapshots that conventional order-parameter analysis had not anticipated. The reader leaves asking which side is ground truth when simulator and variational calculation disagree.

**16. The Tokamak as a Bifurcating System**

Fusion supplies the book's clearest case of a bifurcation that must be controlled in real time rather than merely understood, and the chapter deliberately separates the physics from the control claims. It opens with discovery by accident, Wagner and the ASDEX team finding in 1982 that beam-heated divertor discharges above a power threshold jump into a regime of roughly doubled confinement. Theory then made the jump a bifurcation, first with Shaing and Crume in 1989 and with Houlberg in 1990 deriving a discontinuous jump in poloidal rotation and the radial electric field, then with Hinton's thermal confinement bifurcation admitting two stable branches of the heat-transport equation, then with Diamond, Liang, Carreras, and Terry's self-regulating shear flow in which turbulence generates the flow that quenches it, and Eun-jin Kim and Diamond's prediction of an oscillatory zonal-flow precursor that Schmitz and colleagues then observed directly on DIII-D just inside the separatrix. Against that background the chapter assesses the machine-learning layer. Kates-Harbeck, Svyatkovskiy, and Tang's recurrent network claimed cross-machine disruption forecasting, while Rea, Montes, Granetz, and colleagues ran a random forest live in the DIII-D plasma control system for over 900 discharges and built the multi-machine baseline. Control follows prediction, with Degrave, Felici, and Riedmiller's reinforcement-learning agent commanding TCV's magnetic coils, tempered by Tracey and colleagues' candid catalogue of its accuracy and steady-state limitations, and with Seo, Kim, and Kolemen training an agent inside a learned tearing-likelihood model and holding tearing below threshold in live DIII-D experiments. De Vries and the ITER Organization state the constraint that gives it all urgency, since ITER cannot accumulate a training set of its own disruptions. The reader leaves asking whether a disruption is a bifurcation in the same sense that the L-mode to H-mode transition is, given that it is usually the end of a causal chain rather than one parameter crossing a threshold.

**17. Early Warning in Earth Systems**

The final application chapter takes the same question to systems that cannot be rerun, and it is structured as a dispute rather than a result. The theory is critical slowing down, formalized as a generic framework by Scheffer, Carpenter, Dakos, Lenton, and colleagues, anticipated by Dakos and colleagues' analysis of eight abrupt paleoclimate shifts, and confirmed experimentally by Carpenter and colleagues, who destabilized a whole lake over three years by adding a top predator while monitoring an unmanipulated reference lake. Lenton and colleagues named the tipping elements, and Armstrong McKay, Lenton, and colleagues placed several central thresholds inside the Paris temperature range. Bury, Sujith, Scheffer, Lenton, Anand, and Bauch then trained a deep classifier on roughly half a million simulated series labelled by bifurcation type and applied it to sedimentary anoxia, thermoacoustic instability, and paleoclimate records. The chapter's spine is the Atlantic overturning dispute, where Boers reported critical slowing down in eight observational fingerprints, Ditlevsen and Ditlevsen fitted a saddle-node model and named a collapse window, Chen and Tung argued that expanding observational coverage manufactures the variance rise and that two proxy signs were reversed, Ben-Yami, Skiba, Bathiany, and Boers showed gap-filling can fabricate slowing down while finding a significant residual, and van Westen, Kliphuis, and Dijkstra proposed a physics-based indicator grounded in salt-advection freshwater transport instead. Ashwin, Wieczorek, Vitolo, and Cox supply the taxonomy that makes the dispute tractable, since only bifurcation-induced tipping reliably slows down, and Ditlevsen and Johnsen found no slowing before Dansgaard-Oeschger events at all. The reader leaves asking whether any observable can distinguish bifurcation-induced from noise-induced and rate-induced tipping using data alone.

**18. What Was Actually Discovered**

Chapters twelve through seventeen reported claims, and this chapter tests them, which is why it is a separate chapter rather than a caveat inside each. Its argument is that the field's characteristic failure is not fabrication but a category error, mistaking a well-fitted classifier for a physical discovery. Cheetham and Seshadri examined randomized subsets of the GNoME stable-structure database, identified all ten randomly selected entries in the ICSD, found a space-group distribution unlike reality, since Pnma and P2(1)/c together account for roughly sixteen percent of ICSD structures while Pnma ranks sixteenth in GNoME at 1.56 percent and the four commonest GNoME groups are all non-centrosymmetric, and flagged tens of thousands of compounds containing promethium, actinium, protactinium, technetium, neptunium, or plutonium. Leeman, Liu, Schoop, Palgrave, and colleagues identified systematic Rietveld errors in the A-Lab analysis and argued that roughly two thirds of the claimed successes were known compositionally disordered phases, because zero-temperature calculations neglect configurational entropy, and the 2026 Nature Author Correction retitled the paper from novel to inorganic materials and revised the count to 36 of 57. LK-99 supplies the parallel case, with Jain attributing the resistivity drop to the superionic transition of a copper sulphide impurity at 104 degrees Celsius and Puphal and colleagues showing that actual single crystals are transparent insulators. The methodological counterparts are Bartel's stability finding, Matbench Discovery's metric misalignment, Spangher and Rea's DisruptionBench showing that reported disruption accuracies are not comparable across papers, Arnold and Schäfer's closed-form replacements, and Dablander and Bury's demonstration that a detrending filter choice carries the deep tipping classifier's apparent advantage. The reader leaves asking what a discovery-grade standard of evidence would look like, and who audits it.

**19. Machines That Produce Theory**

If chapter eighteen is the low point of the arc, this is the turn, because it covers systems whose output is an explanation rather than a prediction. The symbolic lineage begins with Schmidt and Lipson recovering Lagrangians and Hamiltonians from raw motion-tracking data by scoring candidate expressions on partial-derivative relationships, continues through Udrescu and Tegmark's AI Feynman, which added dimensional analysis, symmetry detection, and recursive decomposition to solve all one hundred equations of a Feynman benchmark and then, in its second version, returned Pareto frontiers trading accuracy against description length. Cranmer's PySR made the search a standard tool, and the decisive recipe came from Cranmer, Sanchez-Gonzalez, Battaglia, and colleagues, who imposed a sparse bottleneck on a graph network's messages and then symbolically regressed those messages, a method Lemos and colleagues later used to rediscover the inverse-square law and the masses of the solar system's bodies from thirty years of trajectories. Cornelio and colleagues at IBM added the missing criterion with AI-Descartes, scoring a candidate not only on fit but on derivability from background axioms, and Brunton, Proctor, and Kutz's sparse identification recovers governing equations from a candidate library. The coarse-graining lineage is the one that bears directly on this book, with Mehta and Schwab proposing that stacked restricted Boltzmann machines implement variational renormalization, Koch-Janusz and Ringel rejecting that reading and instead maximizing real-space mutual information to extract relevant degrees of freedom and the Ising exponent, Gökmen, Ringel, Huber, and Koch-Janusz scaling this into RSMI-NE, which reconstructs phase diagrams and identifies symmetry-breaking patterns without prior knowledge, and de Mello Koch and colleagues constructing observables that distinguish renormalization flow from what a trained network actually does. The reader leaves asking whether any version of the claim that deep learning is renormalization survives.

**20. The Learner as a Physical System, and What Comes Next**

The book ends by turning its own subject on the tool, and the argument is that in machine learning the phase-transition language is not a metaphor. Hopfield's recurrent network of two-state neurons descends a Lyapunov energy identical in form to an Ising energy, making statistical mechanics literally applicable, and Ackley, Hinton, and Sejnowski's Boltzmann machine imported the equilibrium distribution and a learning rule from the difference between clamped and free-running correlations. Amit, Gutfreund, and Sompolinsky then computed the phase diagram and located the memory cliff at roughly 0.138 patterns per neuron, above which retrieval states vanish into a spin glass, and Gardner shifted the question from the learning rule to the geometry of weight space, computing the volume of admissible couplings and, with Derrida, the optimal capacity of two patterns per weight, more than an order of magnitude above the Hebbian value, work done before she was thirty and before her death in 1988. Sompolinsky, Tishby, and Seung and, independently, Györgyi showed that networks with discrete weights can jump discontinuously to perfect generalization, which is the rigorous ancestor of grokking as Power, Burda, and colleagues later reported it and as Nanda, Chan, and colleagues reverse-engineered it into three continuous phases beneath an apparently abrupt curve. The 2024 Nobel to Hopfield and Hinton formalized the lineage, and the modern chapters of it are Zdeborová and Krzakala's thresholds separating impossible, hard, and easy inference, Geiger, Spigler, Wyart, and colleagues' jamming transition separating under- from over-parametrization, Belkin's double descent, and the dispute between Wei and colleagues on emergent abilities and Schaeffer, Miranda, and Koyejo's argument that discontinuous metrics manufacture them. The reader leaves with the question the book cannot answer, what the order parameter for a transformer would be, and therefore whether these systems have a phase diagram at all.

On waste, four bodies of verified material would be underused by this architecture and the editor should decide deliberately rather than by omission. First, the physics-constrained surrogate lineage, Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis's physics-informed networks demonstrated on the Allen-Cahn equation, Mattey and Ghosh's sequential scheme for Allen-Cahn and Cahn-Hilliard, Krishnapriyan and colleagues' demonstration that the failures are optimization-landscape rather than expressivity failures, and the Fourier Neural Operator, is fully verified and receives only a paragraph in chapter nineteen, because it enforces known physics rather than discovering new physics. Second, the quantum chemistry branch of neural-network states, PauliNet, FermiNet, the Psiformer excited-state work, DeepQMC, and the separate DM21 functional, is a large verified body that chapter nine reduces to a sentence, since the book's spine is phase transitions rather than molecules. Third, percolation and the CALPHAD tradition each appear once despite carrying enough verified material for sustained treatment. Fourth, the ptychography thread, PtychoNN and its edge deployment, and the LK-99 episode are both vivid and both marginal to the argument, LK-99 in particular being a superconductivity dispute that earns its place only as a cautionary case in chapter eighteen.

On gaps, the dossiers are thin in places the architecture needs. There is no primary source for Kibble or Zurek despite two chapters relying on Kibble-Zurek scaling, and Millis 1993, the Fisher and Huse droplet picture, Guerra, Talagrand, Rushbrooke, and the conformal bootstrap results for the three-dimensional Ising model are all named secondhand without verified items, which is a citation problem for chapters two, three, and four specifically. Biological phase separation and condensates are absent entirely, as are non-equilibrium and absorbing-state transitions, directed percolation, and active matter, so the book as designed covers equilibrium and driven macroscopic systems but not the fastest-growing area of soft matter. The physical glass and jamming transitions are absent except as they appear inside learning theory, which is an awkward asymmetry given chapter twenty. Superconductivity machine learning is represented by a single item plus the SuperCon database, too thin to support the chapter that material invites. Generative modelling of thermal configurations beyond crystals is thin, resting on Boltzmann generators and the crystal diffusion models alone. And the dossiers themselves flag two absences that chapters eight, thirteen, and eighteen would each want, namely the lack of any benchmark of experimental diffraction patterns with ground-truth labels, and the lack of any published quantification of false-positive rates for early-warning classifiers on real geophysical records rather than held-out simulations. Finally, a procedural note. I received eleven domain dossiers legibly, the twelfth being cut off partway through the symbolic-theory item list, so chapters nineteen and twenty may need rebalancing once the missing material is supplied.
