# Chapter 1. The Sharpest Thing in Nature

Water on a hotplate at one atmosphere climbs to about 373 kelvin and then stops climbing. The element keeps delivering energy at the same rate, the thermometer keeps reading the same number, and it goes on reading it until the last of the liquid is gone. The energy that enters the water during that plateau is several hundred times what it takes to warm the same water by a single kelvin, and none of it raises the temperature. Then the pan is dry and the reading resumes its climb.

Two properties of that plateau are hard to account for. The first is that it exists at all: energy is absorbed while the control parameter does not move, so the entropy rises by a finite amount at a single value of the temperature. The second is sharper. The plateau begins at a point rather than over an interval. The density falls by about three orders of magnitude between liquid and vapour, and no homogeneous state of intermediate density is stable in between. Between the two phases there is nothing.

That second property has to be earned. Nothing in the microscopic description of a water molecule is discontinuous. The intermolecular potential is smooth, the Boltzmann factor is smooth, the sum over configurations is a sum of smooth positive terms. A discontinuity has to be manufactured from ingredients that contain none, and the machinery that does it works only when the number of molecules is infinite. That is not a technicality; it is why the subject is hard, and why applying machine learning to it is a stranger enterprise than it appears.

## Coexistence as an equality between surfaces

The framework in which the plateau becomes a theorem rather than an observation was supplied by Josiah Willard Gibbs. The memoir "On the equilibrium of heterogeneous substances," published in the Transactions of the Connecticut Academy of Arts and Sciences across 1876 and 1878 and summarized in a long abstract in the American Journal of Science in 1878, defines the chemical potential, derives the phase rule, and introduces the interfacial free energy.

Take the Gibbs free energy $G(T,p)$ of a homogeneous phase of $N$ particles. Its first derivatives are the entropy and the volume,

$$S = -\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_p, \qquad V = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial p}\right)_T .$$

Since $G$ is extensive while both natural variables are intensive, it is proportional to $N$ at fixed $T$ and $p$, and the chemical potential is the free energy per particle, $\mu(T,p) = G(T,p)/N$, a form that compares phases of different size directly.

Now consider two candidate phases, each with its own analytic $\mu(T,p)$. Equilibrium selects whichever surface lies lower, and coexistence is the locus where they cross, $\mu_{\text{liq}} = \mu_{\text{vap}}$, a curve in the plane. The free energy of the real system is the lower envelope of both surfaces, and two smooth surfaces crossing transversally have an envelope with a crease along the crossing. The envelope is continuous; its gradient is not.

No single phase loses analyticity. Analyticity is lost by the operation of taking a minimum over phases, and everything later in the subject is a variation on that sentence. The crease is where the plateau comes from: crossing the curve swaps which surface is being differentiated, and the two have different slopes there, so the entropy jumps by $\Delta S$ and the volume by $\Delta V$. The latent heat is $L = T\,\Delta S$, absorbed at the fixed $T$ of the crossing.

The slope of the coexistence curve follows from the same equality. Per particle, $d\mu = -s\,dT + v\,dp$; coexistence holds all along the curve, so $-s_{\text{liq}}\,dT + v_{\text{liq}}\,dp = -s_{\text{vap}}\,dT + v_{\text{vap}}\,dp$ there. Rearranging gives the ratio of the per-particle jumps, and since both carry the same factor $N$ the extensive jumps may be substituted without changing it,

$$\frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta V} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta V}.$$

This is the Clausius-Clapeyron relation, and it is why the boiling temperature moves when the kettle is carried up a mountain.

Gibbs supplies the geometry of coexistence but not its existence. Nothing above says a crossing must occur, and deriving two branches from a microscopic model proved far harder.

## Ehrenfest's classification, and what survives of it

Gibbs's construction describes a discontinuity in a first derivative. Nothing forbids a transition whose first derivatives are continuous and whose misbehaviour lies further down, and Paul Ehrenfest proposed the scheme that organizes those cases. In "Phasenumwandlungen im ueblichen und erweiterten Sinn, classifiziert nach den entsprechenden Singularitaeten des thermodynamischen Potentiales," published in the Proceedings of the Royal Academy of Sciences at Amsterdam in 1933, Ehrenfest classified transitions by the order of the lowest discontinuous derivative of the thermodynamic potential. Tilman Sauer published an English translation with commentary in 2017.

The first category is the case already described, a discontinuity in a first derivative, hence latent heat. The second was defined by analogy, with continuous first derivatives but a finite jump in the second, meaning a jump in the specific heat, the compressibility or the thermal expansion coefficient. Higher orders follow by extension.

The first category is exactly right, and it has outlived its decade because it is a statement about the analytic structure of the free energy that needs no model and no dimension. The trouble begins one step up. Ehrenfest's construction assumes the offending derivative jumps by a finite amount, and real transitions of that kind do not oblige. The specific heat measured near the lambda point separating the two liquid phases of helium does not step to another value, and the historical record read those measurements as a logarithmic divergence. Sauer's commentary notes that this forced a modification of the scheme, while arguing that the framework proved fruitful and remains important. Both halves hold: the scheme is still the standard entry point, and its higher categories describe a class with no typical members.

Modern usage therefore keeps the first category and replaces the rest with a single word. A transition is first-order if a first derivative of the potential is discontinuous, and continuous otherwise. What does the work is no longer which derivative jumps but whether the singularity is a jump or a divergence.

## The order parameter

Ehrenfest's scheme says where the free energy misbehaves. It does not say what is physically different on the two sides. Lev Davidovich Landau supplied that in "K teorii fazovykh perekhodov," published in Zhurnal Eksperimental'noi i Teoreticheskoi Fiziki in 1937, and the idea has organized the subject ever since.

Landau's proposal was to identify a quantity, the order parameter $\eta$, that vanishes identically in the high-symmetry phase and is nonzero in the low-symmetry phase. Near a continuous transition $\eta$ is small, so the free energy may be expanded in powers of it, and symmetry restricts which powers appear. If the free energy is invariant under $\eta \to -\eta$ the expansion begins at second order,

$$F(T,\eta) = F_0(T) + a\,(T - T_c)\,\eta^2 + b\,\eta^4 + \dots$$

with $a$ and $b$ positive. Above $T_c$ the coefficient of $\eta^2$ is positive and the minimum sits at $\eta = 0$. Below $T_c$ that coefficient turns negative and the single minimum splits into two, at

$$\eta = \pm\sqrt{\frac{a\,(T_c - T)}{2b}} \;\propto\; (T_c - T)^{1/2}.$$

The order parameter grows continuously from zero, so no first derivative jumps and there is no latent heat, yet the state below $T_c$ differs qualitatively from the state above it. The system must choose one of the two minima, and in choosing it breaks a symmetry the free energy still possesses. From the same expansion Landau obtained the exponents now labelled mean-field, $\beta = 1/2$ for the order parameter, $\gamma = 1$ for the susceptibility, $\delta = 3$ for the critical isotherm, and $\alpha = 0$ with a finite jump in the specific heat, together with the rule that a continuous transition requires the symmetry group of the low-symmetry phase to be a subgroup of the high-symmetry phase's. Symmetry cannot be turned down gradually, so where no such containment exists the transition is first-order or does not happen.

## Where the expansion fails

Landau's expansion carries an assumption that is false at precisely the point of interest. It treats the free energy as an analytic function of the order parameter at the transition, and the transition is where analyticity is unavailable.

The expansion evaluates the free energy at a single uniform value of $\eta$, the most probable one. That is a saddle-point approximation, reliable when fluctuations about the saddle are small, and near a continuous transition they are not. The correlation length diverges, so the order parameter fluctuates coherently over regions of every size up to $\xi$, and all those scales contribute. The comparison that settles the matter takes the mean-square fluctuation averaged over one correlation volume and sets it against the square of the mean-field order parameter. Using the mean-field values $\beta = 1/2$ and $\nu = 1/2$, the fluctuation term scales as $\xi^{2-d} \sim |t|^{(d-2)/2}$ and the mean-field term as $|t|^{2\beta} = |t|$, so the ratio of neglected to retained is proportional to $|t|^{(d-4)/2}$. Below four spatial dimensions that exponent is negative, the ratio grows without bound as the transition is approached, and the discarded term eventually dominates the retained one. Above four the ratio vanishes and the saddle point becomes asymptotically exact. Four is not a round number chosen for convenience; it is where that exponent changes sign.

The failure below four dimensions cannot be repaired by keeping more terms. What is being expanded is an average over fluctuations up to a diverging length, and no polynomial in a uniform $\eta$ represents that. The criterion says where mean-field theory is wrong without saying what is right instead, and supplying that took another three decades.

## Evidence that the classical exponents are wrong

The lattice models proved the problem was real rather than technical. Wilhelm Lenz proposed the model that now carries the name of Ernst Ising, the doctoral student who solved its one-dimensional version in 1925 and found no phase transition at any nonzero temperature; Ising extrapolated that conclusion to higher dimensions, incorrectly. Rudolf Peierls gave the first argument that the two-dimensional model orders at low temperature, in 1936, by bounding the entropy of domain-wall contours against their energy cost. Lars Onsager obtained the exact partition function of the two-dimensional square lattice in zero field in 1944, demonstrating a continuous transition with a logarithmically diverging specific heat and the exact critical temperature $\sinh(2J/k T_c) = 1$, that is $k T_c/J = 2/\ln(1+\sqrt{2}) \approx 2.269$. Chen Ning Yang published the first derivation of the spontaneous magnetization in 1952, giving $\beta = 1/8$; Onsager had announced the result in 1948 without publishing a proof. One eighth is not one half, and a logarithmic divergence is not a finite jump, so a smooth microscopic Hamiltonian had produced critical behaviour beyond the reach of any mean-field calculation.

Experiment had said the same thing in between, and more broadly. Edward Armand Guggenheim showed in 1945 that the liquid-gas coexistence curves of neon, argon, krypton, xenon, nitrogen, oxygen, carbon monoxide and methane collapse onto a single curve when scaled by their critical parameters, fitted by an exponent near $1/3$ rather than the van der Waals mean-field $1/2$. Eight chemically unrelated substances sharing one curve is universality, observed a quarter century before there was a theory of it.

## Scaling, and the flow that explains it

The first repairs were phenomenological. G. S. Rushbrooke established in 1963 the inequality $\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma \ge 2$ between measurable exponents. Benjamin Widom proposed in 1965 that the singular part of the free energy is a generalized homogeneous function of temperature and field, from which the relations among exponents follow as equalities rather than inequalities, including $\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = 2$ and the Widom relation $\gamma = \beta(\delta - 1)$. Strengthening homogeneity to the statement that the singular free energy per unit volume scales as $\xi^{-d}$ gives the hyperscaling relation $2 - \alpha = d\nu$, hence $\nu = 1$ in two dimensions. The hypothesis reproduced the measured relations without explaining why any free energy should be homogeneous.

Leo Philip Kadanoff supplied a rationale in 1966 with the block-spin construction, coarse-graining the lattice into cells larger than the spacing and smaller than the correlation length and arguing that the result is again an Ising model with rescaled couplings. If so, the theory looks the same at every scale between the two cutoffs, and homogeneity is the shadow of that fact.

Kenneth Geddes Wilson turned the picture into a method in a pair of 1971 papers. Write the coarse-graining as a map on the space of coupling constants: integrating out short-wavelength degrees of freedom and rescaling lengths by a factor $b$ carries a Hamiltonian with couplings $\{g_i\}$ to one with couplings $\{g_i'\}$, and iterating generates a flow. A critical point is a fixed point of that flow, a Hamiltonian that reproduces itself under coarse-graining, which is what scale invariance amounts to once written as an operation rather than asserted as a property.

Near the fixed point the map may be linearized, and its eigenvalues, conventionally written $b^{y_i}$, sort the directions in coupling space. Displacements along a direction with $y_i > 0$ are amplified by coarse-graining and carry the system away from criticality; these are the relevant directions. Displacements with $y_i < 0$ shrink to nothing and are irrelevant. The exponents follow from the relevant eigenvalues in one step. Coarse-graining measures lengths in units $b$ times larger, so $\xi(\{g\}) = b\,\xi(\{g'\})$; at the Ising fixed point the two relevant directions are the reduced temperature and the field, with $t' = b^{y_t} t$; iterating until $b^{y_t}|t|$ reaches order unity, where $\xi$ is a few lattice spacings, gives $\xi \sim |t|^{-1/y_t}$, so $\nu = 1/y_t$, and the field eigenvalue supplies the rest. In this framework an exponent is not fitted to data, it is a property of the linearization of a map.

Universality is then a matter of counting. A fixed point typically has few relevant directions and infinitely many irrelevant ones, so two Hamiltonians differing only along irrelevant directions are carried to the same fixed point and acquire the same eigenvalues. The differences between argon and a uniaxial ferromagnet are irrelevant in exactly this technical sense, and what survives the flow is all the exponents can depend on.

Whether a suitable fixed point existed in three dimensions was, before 1972, largely conjecture. Wilson and Michael Ellis Fisher made it calculable in that year by treating the spatial dimension as a continuous parameter. Dimension enters the perturbation theory only through momentum integrals, defined for non-integer $d$ by their formulae rather than by any underlying lattice, so the theory may be continued to $d = 4 - \epsilon$. At $d = 4$, the upper critical dimension the fluctuation argument identified, mean-field theory becomes exact. Just below four the quartic coupling turns relevant with an eigenvalue of order $\epsilon$, and the flow acquires a nontrivial fixed point at a coupling of the same order, so weak coupling is guaranteed by the smallness of $\epsilon$ rather than assumed, and the exponents emerge as power series in it. Three dimensions is $\epsilon = 1$, which is not small, so the series is asymptotic and is used far outside the region where it was derived, justified after the fact by landing close enough to measured exponents to be useful. Fisher's 1974 review sets out what the resulting classification depends on, namely spatial dimension, the number of order-parameter components, and the range and anisotropy of the interactions. Wilson alone received the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physics for the theory of critical phenomena in connection with phase transitions; Fisher and Kadanoff did not share it, though the three had jointly received the Wolf Prize in Physics in 1980.

A continuous transition, in the picture that survived all this, is a point at which the correlation length diverges,

$$\xi \sim |t|^{-\nu}, \qquad t \equiv \frac{T - T_c}{T_c},$$

taking the system to a state with no characteristic length, and at which the response functions diverge as their own powers of $|t|$. Universality is also the only reason to expect an exponent measured on one system to say anything about another, which is what the last section of this chapter turns on.

## No singularity fits in a finite box

Consider any finite system: $N$ spins, or $N$ particles in a finite volume, with bounded energies. The canonical partition function

$$Z_N(\beta) = \sum_{\{s\}} e^{-\beta E(\{s\})}$$

is a finite sum of exponentials. Each term is entire in $\beta$ and strictly positive for real $\beta$, so $Z_N$ is entire with no real zeros; therefore $\ln Z_N$ is real-analytic in $\beta$, and so is $F_N = -k_B T \ln Z_N$. Every derivative of $F_N$ exists and is finite at every real temperature. There is no latent heat, no diverging specific heat, no diverging correlation length. A finite system has no phase transition whatsoever, in the strict sense of the word.

The singularity is manufactured by the limit, and the mechanism is exact. It belongs to the theory of the zeros of the partition function in the complex plane, and the version used here goes under complex-temperature zeros, which is the term to search. The energies of a finite lattice take values in an evenly spaced set, so in the variable $x = e^{-\beta\epsilon}$, with $\epsilon$ the spacing, $Z_N$ is a polynomial of finite degree times a fixed power of $x$, and the polynomial factorizes over its roots,

$$Z_N(x) = c\,x^{-m} \prod_j (x - x_j), \qquad \ln Z_N(x) = \ln c - m \ln x + \sum_j \ln(x - x_j).$$

The power of $x$ is analytic for $x > 0$ and contributes nothing singular, and each remaining logarithm is singular at its own root and analytic elsewhere. The positivity argument says only that no root lies on the positive real axis; it says nothing about how close to that axis the roots may come. For finite $N$ they sit off the axis at some nonzero distance, and $\ln Z_N$ is analytic in a strip whose width that distance sets. As $N$ grows the roots become more numerous and crowd toward the axis. A phase transition is the point at which, in the limit, two arcs of roots pinch the axis and close the strip. The character of the failure is fixed by how the roots arrive: a nonvanishing line density meeting the axis produces a jump in the first derivative, which is a first-order transition, and a density vanishing as the axis is approached produces the milder power-law singularity of a continuous one.

The singularity is therefore present in the finite system rather than conjured from nothing at the end. It sits as a set of complex points that never touch the real axis, and every quantity a laboratory or a simulation can measure is evaluated on that axis, where the free energy is analytic. Gibbs's crease, Ehrenfest's discontinuous derivative, Landau's symmetry breaking and Onsager's logarithmic divergence describe an idealized object that no sample and no simulation instantiates.

What a finite system shows instead is a rounded crossover, and the rounding follows from the correlation length alone. In the infinite system $\xi$ grows as $|t|^{-\nu}$, but in a box of linear size $L$ it cannot exceed $L$, so the system stops behaving as though it were infinite once $\xi$ reaches the walls, at a reduced temperature

$$|t^*| \sim L^{-1/\nu}.$$

Inside that window the divergence is cut off. Substituting the window width into the specific-heat power law gives a peak of finite height growing as $L^{\alpha/\nu}$, displaced from the true $T_c$ by an amount of order $L^{-1/\nu}$. The two-dimensional Ising model must be read with care here, since $\alpha = 0$ there and $L^0$ is a constant. What $\alpha = 0$ means is a logarithm rather than the absence of a divergence: cutting off $\ln|t|$ at $|t^*| \sim L^{-1/\nu}$ gives a peak growing as $(1/\nu)\ln L$, which with $\nu = 1$ is $\ln L$. A first-order transition rounds for a different reason and lands in the same place, over a width of order $1/N$. Larger systems have narrower crossovers, and no system has a singularity.

In computational statistical physics this is a tool rather than an obstacle. The dependence of the crossover on $L$ is itself the signal, and $T_c$ and the exponents are extracted by fitting the drift of finite-size data against an assumed scaling form. The practice is sound, and it is worth being clear about what it does: it recovers the singularity by assuming the functional form the singularity would have and fitting parameters into it. The infinite-system answer is not read off the data, it is imported as an ansatz and calibrated by them.

## A smooth machine for a sharp world

Now put a neural network into that setting. Here is the procedure this book implements in a later chapter, on the two-dimensional Ising model. Configurations are sampled by Monte Carlo from a finite lattice across a range of temperatures, each labelled by whether its temperature lay above or below a known $T_c$. A classifier is trained to reproduce the labels. Its output is averaged over the configurations sampled at each temperature, and the temperature at which that average crosses one half is reported as an estimate of $T_c$. What that number estimates is a question the finite-box argument mostly settles before any code is written.

One line of attack can be dismissed at once. Nothing follows from the smoothness of the network in its inputs, because the inputs are points of the finite set $\{-1,+1\}^N$, where there are no limits to take and a smooth interpolator and a lookup table are the same object.

What varies continuously is the temperature, and there the argument is short and exact. The quantity plotted against temperature is the average of the network output over configurations drawn from the Boltzmann distribution of a finite lattice,

$$\langle f_\theta \rangle_\beta = \frac{\sum_{\{s\}} f_\theta(s)\,e^{-\beta E(\{s\})}}{\sum_{\{s\}} e^{-\beta E(\{s\})}}.$$

Numerator and denominator are finite sums of exponentials with fixed coefficients, both entire in $\beta$, and the denominator is strictly positive for real $\beta$, so the ratio is real-analytic by exactly the argument that made $\ln Z_N$ analytic. The complex zeros of the denominator stay off the real axis, and no finite lattice brings them onto it. This holds whatever $f_\theta$ is: a network with smooth activations, a network with rectified linear units, a step function of a single spin, any function at all on a finite configuration space. The crossing of one half is the crossing of an analytic curve, analytic for reasons that have nothing to do with the model and everything to do with the finiteness of the lattice.

Real-analytic is stronger than smooth, and the gap decides what may be inferred from such a curve. The function $\exp(-1/x^2)$, extended by zero at the origin, is smooth everywhere and analytic everywhere except the origin, where all its derivatives vanish. A smooth function can therefore lie flat over an interval and then rise, concealing on any neighbourhood what it is about to do; an analytic function cannot, since its values on the whole axis are fixed by its behaviour on any neighbourhood, however small. The curve $\langle f_\theta \rangle_\beta$ hides no singularity waiting at $T_c$, and no amount of resolution near the crossing will find one.

The situation is not that a smooth model fails to represent a singularity the data contains. Configurations drawn from a lattice of ten thousand spins belong to a system whose free energy is analytic everywhere, whose correlation length never exceeds a hundred lattice spacings, and whose behaviour near $T_c$ is a smooth crossover of finite width. Model and data are matched. Both are analytic, neither contains a singularity, and the number being reported describes an object present in neither.

Something is nonetheless being estimated. A trained classifier defines a decision surface in configuration space and a temperature at which typical configurations cross it, and that temperature depends on the sampling, on the finite size, and on the inductive bias of the architecture. My expectation is that the second dominates, so that the crossing is a finite-size crossover of the same species as the specific-heat peak, displaced from the true $T_c$ by an amount of order $L^{-1/\nu}$, which is $L^{-1}$ in two dimensions. Holding the protocol fixed and varying $L$ tests it: if the crossing drifts at the rate the correlation length dictates, and drifts that way whatever the architecture, that is universality doing its ordinary work, and the network is an expensive route to a number the specific heat already supplies. If the drift depends on the architecture, the reported number is partly a property of the network, and agreement with Onsager is a coincidence with no reason to recur.

Either way the classifier answers some question, and it is not the one the headline number implies. It reports the temperature at which typical configurations stop resembling those it was shown as cold, judged by a surface fitted to a sample rather than derived from a Hamiltonian. Whether that has anything to do with the point where an infinite lattice would lose its analyticity is a question about the classifier as much as about the lattice, and it cannot be settled by checking the answer against Onsager, because the problems worth pointing a network at are exactly the ones with no Onsager to check against.
