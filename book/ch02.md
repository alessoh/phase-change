# Chapter 2. Universality, and the Art of Throwing Things Away

In 1945 Edward Armand Guggenheim published a figure that has no business working. On a single pair of axes it carries the liquid-gas coexistence curves of eight substances: neon, argon, krypton, xenon, nitrogen, oxygen, carbon monoxide and methane. Monatomic noble gases sit beside diatomic molecules and a tetrahedral hydrocarbon, and the forces between constituents differ in strength and in character. Yet when the temperature of each substance is divided by its own critical temperature, and the density by its own critical density, the eight curves lie on top of one another, within the scatter of the measurements.

Guggenheim also read an exponent off the common curve. Near the critical point the difference between liquid and vapour densities vanishes as a power of the distance from the critical temperature, and the power that fits is close to $1/3$. The van der Waals equation predicts $1/2$, as does every other theory of its type then available.

The collapse says that the details of a substance stop mattering near a critical point. The number says that whatever replaces those details escapes the obvious approximation. Both answers arrived together, and the vehicle for both is a lattice of arrows that point up or down.

## A model with almost nothing in it

Wilhelm Lenz proposed the model. Ernst Ising, Lenz's doctoral student at the University of Hamburg, solved it and published the result in 1925. Place a variable $s_i = \pm 1$ on each site $i$ of a regular lattice, let neighbours prefer to agree, and let a field bias the system toward one sign:

$$H = -J \sum_{\langle ij \rangle} s_i s_j - h \sum_i s_i,$$

with $J > 0$ rewarding alignment. A real magnet has orbital structure, lattice vibrations, conduction electrons and relativistic corrections. All of it has been deleted. What survives is a two-valued degree of freedom, a local preference for agreement, and a temperature.

In one dimension the model can be solved completely, and Ising solved it. The free energy is analytic at every nonzero temperature and there is no transition. A reversed segment costs a fixed energy however long it is, since only its two endpoints break bonds, while the number of places to put those endpoints grows with the chain. Entropy wins at any positive temperature.

Ising then extrapolated, and here the story acquires its most persistent myth. The conclusion drawn was that the model has no transition in higher dimensions either, which is false and which no one demonstrated. The one-dimensional solution is the paper's real content, and the model itself belongs to Lenz.

The same lattice describes a fluid. Let each site be occupied or empty, with $n_i \in \{0,1\}$, let occupied neighbours attract with energy $-w$ per pair, and let a chemical potential $\mu$ set the mean density. Substituting $n_i = (1 + s_i)/2$ returns the Ising energy exactly, with

$$J = \frac{w}{4}, \qquad h = \frac{\mu}{2} + \frac{qw}{4},$$

where $q$ is the number of neighbours of a site, plus one configuration-independent constant. The two partition functions agree up to a known prefactor, which cancels from every derivative that defines an exponent. This is a change of variables, not an analogy. Density plays the part of magnetisation, so the exponent Guggenheim fitted to eight fluids is the order-parameter exponent of the lattice model. The mapping has a boundary worth marking. Exchanging occupied and empty sites is the spin-flip symmetry of the magnet, so the lattice gas is exactly symmetric about half filling, while nothing forces a real fluid to be symmetric about its critical density. What the mapping delivers is the exponent, not the shape of the whole curve.

## The argument that rescued it

Rudolf Peierls gave the first argument, in 1936, that the two-dimensional model does order at low temperature, and it is still what textbooks use. In two dimensions a reversed region is bounded by a closed contour of broken bonds, so its cost grows with its size. A contour of length $L$ costs energy $2JL$ and carries weight $e^{-2\beta J L}$, while the number of contours of that length enclosing a site grows only exponentially in $L$, so entropy also contributes a term of order $L$ in the exponent. At low enough temperature the energy wins for every $L$ at once, the total weight of contours enclosing a site can be bounded below one half, and the magnetisation survives. Dimension decides the question.

## The exact answer, and what it cost the classical picture

Lars Onsager, at Yale University, obtained the exact partition function of the two-dimensional square-lattice model in zero field in 1944. The transition is genuinely continuous, and the critical temperature comes out in closed form:

$$\sinh\!\left(\frac{2J}{k T_c}\right) = 1, \qquad \frac{k T_c}{J} = \frac{2}{\ln(1 + \sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269 .$$

The specific heat does not jump at $T_c$; it diverges logarithmically.

Set against this is the framework that had organised the subject, built by Lev Davidovich Landau in 1937. Landau's theory uses an order parameter that vanishes in the symmetric phase, expands the free energy in powers of it, and requires the low-symmetry phase's symmetry group to be a subgroup of the high-symmetry one. Minimising $f = f_0 + a t \phi^2 + b \phi^4 - h\phi$ over $\phi$, with $t = (T - T_c)/T_c$, gives $\phi \sim (-t)^{1/2}$ below the transition.

With $t$ the reduced distance from $T_c$ and $h$ the ordering field, the specific heat behaves as $C \sim |t|^{-\alpha}$. The order parameter, the magnetisation of a magnet or the density difference of a fluid, vanishes as $m \sim (-t)^{\beta}$, and its response to the field diverges as $\chi \sim |t|^{-\gamma}$. On the critical isotherm the order parameter follows the field as $m \sim h^{1/\delta}$. Two exponents concern lengths: the correlation length diverges as $\xi \sim |t|^{-\nu}$, and at the critical point the correlation between spins a distance $r$ apart decays as $1/r^{d-2+\eta}$ rather than the naive $1/r^{d-2}$, which defines the anomalous dimension $\eta$. A last one, $\omega$, controls the leading correction to pure power-law behaviour and limits the precision of any calculation. A primed exponent denotes the approach to $T_c$ from below, so $\alpha'$ and $\gamma'$ are in principle independent of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$. The symbol $\beta$ is overloaded, being also the inverse temperature of the Peierls weight above and the name of the flow functions below.

Landau's expansion gives $\beta = 1/2$, $\gamma = 1$, $\delta = 3$, and $\alpha = 0$ in the sense of a finite jump in the specific heat. That is exactly the second-derivative jump of the second-order category Paul Ehrenfest had proposed when classifying transitions by the lowest discontinuous derivative of the thermodynamic potential. A logarithmic divergence is not a finite jump, which is why Ehrenfest's higher-order categories do not describe real continuous transitions, and why modern usage distinguishes only first-order transitions from continuous ones.

Onsager announced the spontaneous magnetisation of the two-dimensional model in 1948 without publishing a derivation. The first published derivation is due to Chen Ning Yang in 1952, at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, and it gives

$$m \sim (T_c - T)^{1/8}, \qquad \beta = 1/8 .$$

One eighth, against the mean-field one half, and the discrepancy is no artifact of approximation, since neither Onsager's calculation nor Yang's involves one. Three-dimensional fluids give roughly $1/3$, the two-dimensional lattice magnet exactly $1/8$, and the Landau expansion $1/2$ for both. Mean-field theory is not a third physical case standing beside the other two. It is an approximation that fails differently in two dimensions and in three, and what differs is spatial dimension and nothing in the chemistry.

## Relations before explanations

Through the early 1960s the exponents accumulated relations among themselves before anyone could say where the relations came from, and the chronology is often told backwards.

On 15 February 1963, John W. Essam and Michael Ellis Fisher, at the Wheatstone Physics Laboratory of King's College, London, published a study of the lattice gas and the Ising ferromagnet below the critical point. Padé approximant analysis of low-temperature series gave them $0.303 \le \beta \le 0.318$, with $\beta = 5/16$ conjectured, close to what Guggenheim had fitted to eight real fluids eighteen years earlier. The identity in the same paper came from elsewhere. Essam and Fisher credit it to consideration of a heuristic model partition function rather than to the numerics, and it reads

$$\alpha' + 2\beta + \gamma' = 2 .$$

Five and a half months later, on 1 August 1963, George Stanley Rushbrooke published a two-page paper, written from the Chemistry Department at the University of Oregon, proving the corresponding inequality $\alpha' + 2\beta + \gamma' \ge 2$ by rigorous thermodynamic argument and citing Essam and Fisher as its first reference. Thermodynamics could bound the exponents but not pin them. Robert B. Griffiths added a companion inequality in 1965, $\alpha' + \beta(1 + \delta) \ge 2$. Two inequalities, and no explanation of why either should be an equality.

Benjamin Widom supplied the missing assumption later in 1965. Working in the Department of Chemistry at Cornell University, Widom introduced a function $\Phi(x,y)$, with $x$ a measure of the temperature and $y$ a measure of the density, and suggested that in a real fluid $\Phi$ is homogeneous in $x$ and $y$ with a positive degree of homogeneity. The homogeneity is a generalised one, the arguments carrying different weights,

$$\Phi(\lambda^{a} x, \lambda^{b} y) = \lambda \, \Phi(x,y),$$

with the degree on the right unity only by convention, since any positive degree can be rescaled to one. The content lies in two numbers rather than three.

Choosing $\lambda$ to eliminate one argument reduces $\Phi$ to a curve of a single variable together with $a$ and $b$. Every exponent is then some combination of those two numbers, and because there are more exponents than parameters, relations must hold among them. Rushbrooke's inequality and Griffiths' become equalities, and $\gamma = \beta(\delta - 1)$, the Widom relation, follows as well. Fisher, who had been on the other side of the 1963 exchange, reviewed the passage from inequalities to equalities in 1967. What the hypothesis does not do is explain itself. Homogeneity is assumed because it works, and asking why the singular part of a free energy should be generalised homogeneous is the question of why critical points look the way they do, renamed.

## Kadanoff's blocks

Leo Philip Kadanoff answered the renamed question in 1966, at the University of Illinois in Urbana, in a paper published in the short-lived journal Physics Physique Fizika, which is why it is so often miscited. Take the Ising lattice near its critical point, where the correlation length $\xi$ is large compared with the lattice spacing $a$, and partition it into cells of side $ba$ with $a \ll ba \ll \xi$. Inside such a cell the spins are strongly correlated, so the cell behaves as a unit and is replaced by a single block spin.

Kadanoff's claim is that the coarse-grained system is again an Ising model, on a lattice of spacing $ba$, with a rescaled coupling and a rescaled field. Grant it and the consequence is immediate. Coarse-graining does not change the free energy, only how it is counted, and there are $b^d$ times fewer blocks than sites, so the singular part per degree of freedom satisfies

$$f_s(t,h) = b^{-d} f_s\!\left(b^{y_t} t, \; b^{y_h} h\right),$$

with $y_t$ and $y_h$ the powers by which rescaled temperature and field grow under coarse-graining by $b$. This is Widom's generalised homogeneity, derived rather than posited, with $b$ in the role of $\lambda$.

The relations follow in a few lines. The identity holds for every $b$, so choose $b = |t|^{-1/y_t}$, which drives the first argument to $\pm 1$ and removes it, leaving

$$f_s(t,h) = |t|^{d/y_t} \, \Psi\!\left(h\,|t|^{-y_h/y_t}\right)$$

for some $\Psi$ of a single variable. Two derivatives in $t$ at $h = 0$ give $C \sim |t|^{d/y_t - 2}$, so $2 - \alpha = d/y_t$. One derivative in $h$ gives $\beta = (d - y_h)/y_t$, and two give $\gamma = (2y_h - d)/y_t$. Assembled in Rushbrooke's combination, everything cancels:

$$\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = \left(2 - \frac{d}{y_t}\right) + \frac{2d - 2y_h}{y_t} + \frac{2y_h - d}{y_t} = 2 .$$

One step there deserves naming. A single function $\Psi$ governs $t > 0$ and $t < 0$ alike, and the exponents come from the powers of $|t|$ in front of it, which do not know the sign of $t$. Scaling therefore forces $\alpha = \alpha'$ and $\gamma = \gamma'$, and only for that reason may the primes be dropped and the identity read as a statement about the numbers Rushbrooke bounded. An inequality thermodynamics could not close has become an identity.

Lengths obey the same logic. Coarse-graining divides the correlation length, $\xi(t) = b\, \xi(b^{y_t} t)$, and the same choice of $b$ gives $\nu = 1/y_t$. With the specific-heat relation this yields

$$2 - \alpha = d\nu ,$$

which contains the spatial dimension itself and which no thermodynamic argument could produce. It is also the one relation here that is not always true. Every step above assumed that the singular free energy carries no scale but $\xi$, so that the free energy per correlation volume is a pure number with no residual dependence on $b$. That assumption fails above a certain dimension, for a reason that needs the language of the next section.

## The flow

The block-spin picture has a defect: nothing guarantees that the coarse-grained system is exactly an Ising model. Averaging over the interior of a cell generates couplings that were not there before, between next-nearest neighbours and among four spins at a time. The coarse-grained Hamiltonian lives in a space of infinitely many couplings, not the two-dimensional space of $J$ and $h$.

Kenneth Geddes Wilson, at Cornell University, turned that defect into the method. In a pair of companion papers in 1971, Wilson recast coarse-graining as a flow in the full space of couplings $\{g_i\}$ at scale $b = e^{\ell}$. Integrating out short-wavelength degrees of freedom and rescaling produces

$$\frac{d g_i}{d\ell} = \beta_i(\{g\}),$$

and the theory of critical behaviour becomes the qualitative theory of this flow. A critical point is a fixed point, a set of couplings that reproduces itself under coarse-graining, which is the algebraic form of scale invariance. Linearising about it, the eigenvalues $y_i$ classify directions in coupling space. Those with $y_i > 0$ grow and are called relevant. Those with $y_i < 0$ shrink and are called irrelevant. Those with $y_i = 0$ are marginal, and the linearised flow settles nothing about them, since the leading nonlinear term decides whether such a coupling grows or dies. The signature of a marginal direction is a logarithm where a power would otherwise stand.

The exponents are not the eigenvalues, but they are determined by them. The two relevant eigenvalues at the Ising fixed point are the $y_t$ and $y_h$ of the scaling form, so every relation of the previous section is inherited, and the leading irrelevant eigenvalue supplies $\omega$. What the flow adds is a reason why only two directions should be relevant, and a means of computing their eigenvalues.

Universality becomes a statement about basins of attraction. A lattice magnet and a fluid start at different points in coupling space, and if the difference between them lies entirely along irrelevant directions, coarse-graining shrinks it away and both reach the same fixed point. Guggenheim's collapse is then not a coincidence about noble gases. It says that the differences among the eight substances are irrelevant in the technical sense, already discarded by the flow. The renormalisation group is a systematic procedure for deleting information, and its content is the claim that a characterisable part of what it deletes was never doing any work.

## Where four comes from

The dimension at which mean-field theory stops being wrong follows from the Landau expansion itself. Below $T_c$ the minimisation gives $\phi_0^2 \sim |t|$, while Gaussian fluctuations about that minimum have $\xi \sim |t|^{-1/2}$ and a correlation function falling as $1/r^{d-2}$ out to $r \sim \xi$. Averaged over one correlation volume, the mean square fluctuation of the order parameter is

$$\langle \delta\phi^2 \rangle_\xi \sim \xi^{-d}\int_{r < \xi} \frac{d^d r}{r^{d-2}} \sim \xi^{2-d} ,$$

and consistency requires this to be small beside $\phi_0^2$ itself. Since $\phi_0^2 \sim |t| \sim \xi^{-2}$, the ratio of the two is $\xi^{4-d}$, and $\xi$ diverges at the transition. For $d > 4$ the ratio vanishes and mean-field exponents are exact. For $d < 4$ it diverges however close one begins, and the fluctuations the theory neglected dominate the quantity it was computing. This is the Ginzburg criterion, and four is the upper critical dimension it produces. At $d = 4$ the ratio is independent of $\xi$, the marginal case of the previous section, and the corrections are logarithmic.

The same number appears in the flow as the eigenvalue of the quartic coupling, which is $4 - d$ at the Gaussian fixed point. For $d > 4$ that coupling is irrelevant, and this is where hyperscaling breaks. Irrelevant normally means removable, in the sense that the coupling can be set to zero without loss. But the mean-field order parameter is $\phi_0^2 = -at/2b$, which diverges as the quartic coefficient $b$ goes to zero, so the free energy has no finite limit when that coupling is switched off. Such a coupling, irrelevant by its eigenvalue yet impossible to discard inside the scaling function, is called dangerously irrelevant, and it leaves the coarse-grained free energy with a dependence on $b$ that the counting behind $f_s(t,h) = b^{-d}f_s(b^{y_t}t, b^{y_h}h)$ never included. The counting fails, and so does the relation drawn from it. Mean-field theory gives $\alpha = 0$ and $\nu = 1/2$, hence $2 - \alpha = 2$ while $d\nu = d/2$, and the two sides agree only at $d = 4$. Above four dimensions the mean-field exponents are correct and hyperscaling is false. Every other scaling relation survives, since none of the others contains $d$.

Below four dimensions the quartic coupling is relevant at the Gaussian fixed point and the flow carries the system elsewhere. Wilson and Fisher located the new fixed point in 1972 by treating the dimension as a continuous parameter and working at $d = 4 - \epsilon$, where the coupling is itself of order $\epsilon$ and the perturbation theory is therefore controlled. Their paper is titled "Critical Exponents in 3.99 Dimensions." At one loop,

$$\nu = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{12} + O(\epsilon^2),$$

while the anomalous dimension is of order $\epsilon^2$ and does not appear at this order at all. Setting $\epsilon = 1$ at the end, which is not obviously legitimate and works anyway, gives $\nu \approx 0.583$ against a modern three-dimensional value near $0.630$. That is an error of about seven per cent, from one term of a series in a parameter set equal to one. The number matters less than the existence of the series, with each further order defined and computable.

What is left after the deletion is small. Fisher's 1974 review sets out the inventory. A universality class is fixed by the spatial dimensionality $d$, by the number of components $n$ of the order parameter, and by the range and anisotropy of the interactions. The chemistry, the lattice structure and the form of the potential are gone. Recognition was divided unevenly. Wilson, Fisher and Kadanoff shared the Wolf Prize in Physics in 1980, and the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1982 went to Wilson alone, "for his theory for critical phenomena in connection with phase transitions."

## How well the numbers are now known

The three-dimensional Ising universality class is a definite mathematical object, and more than one method computes its exponents to high precision. Martin Hasenbusch established the modern Monte Carlo benchmark in 2010, using finite-size scaling of the spin-$1/2$ Ising and Blume-Capel models on simple cubic lattices up to linear size $360$, obtaining $\nu = 0.63002(10)$, $\eta = 0.03627(10)$ and $\omega = 0.832(6)$. In 2012 Sheer El-Showk, Miguel F. Paulos, David Poland, Slava Rychkov, David Simmons-Duffin and Alessandro Vichi applied the conformal bootstrap to the same model, imposing only symmetry and consistency conditions on the space of possible theories, and found that it lies at a corner point on the boundary of the allowed parameter space.

In 2014 the same six authors sharpened the method and located a sharp transition in the operator spectrum at the scaling dimension $\Delta_\sigma = 0.518154(15)$. A scaling dimension is the power governing how an operator behaves under the rescaling of the previous sections, and in three dimensions the dimensions of the spin operator $\sigma$, the energy operator $\epsilon$ and the leading correction operator $\epsilon'$ convert into exponents through $\Delta_\sigma = 1/2 + \eta/2$, $\Delta_\epsilon = 3 - 1/\nu$ and $\Delta_{\epsilon'} = 3 + \omega$. The subscript $\epsilon$ labels an operator here, not the expansion parameter $4 - d$. With $\Delta_\epsilon = 1.41267(13)$ and $\Delta_{\epsilon'} = 3.8303(18)$, those conversions give $\nu = 0.62999(5)$, $\eta = 0.03631(3)$ and $\omega = 0.8303(18)$, a factor of two to three more precise than the Monte Carlo determinations and consistent with them.

Neither list contains $\beta$, the exponent this chapter began with, and it is not an independent number. Since $\beta = (d - y_h)/y_t$, $\nu = 1/y_t$ and $\Delta_\sigma = d - y_h$, the order-parameter exponent is $\beta = \nu \Delta_\sigma$, which in terms of the quantities already on the page is

$$\beta = \frac{\nu(1 + \eta)}{2} = 0.3264 .$$

Guggenheim fitted something close to $1/3$, or $0.3333$, to eight real fluids in 1945, from measurements alone. Essam and Fisher conjectured $5/16$, or $0.3125$, from low-temperature series in 1963. The modern value falls between them, nearer to Guggenheim's, and nowhere near the mean-field $1/2$ the subject began by contradicting.

The two determinations agree to the digits quoted, and they share no input with one another. Hasenbusch's numbers come from simulating particular lattice Hamiltonians at particular sizes, which is microscopic input and nothing but. The bootstrap numbers come from consistency conditions on a space of conformal field theories, with no Hamiltonian in the calculation and no lattice for one to live on. Agreement between a computation that starts from one specific microscopic model and one that never mentions any is the sharpest evidence available that the exponents belong to the fixed point rather than to either starting point.

## What was thrown away

Philip Warren Anderson, writing in 1972 in what became the canonical citation for emergence in phase-transition writing, argued that accepting reductionism does not commit one to constructionism. Reducing everything to simple fundamental laws does not confer the ability to start from those laws and reconstruct the universe, because at each level of complexity entirely new properties appear, and broken symmetry is the central example. The renormalisation group gives that argument a technical spine. It does not merely assert that higher-level descriptions are autonomous; it builds the map from the lower level to the higher one and identifies what the map destroys.

A structural analogy runs through the rest of this book. Coarse-graining maps a high-dimensional configuration space repeatedly onto a much smaller space of effective couplings, preserving what one cares about and compressing the rest away. Representation learning also maps a high-dimensional input space onto a smaller one in stages, with the compression determined by what the training objective demands be preserved. In both cases the interesting object is the structure that survives the map rather than the map itself.

The analogy has a limit, and the limit is where this book's argument begins. In the physics case the criterion for relevance is derived. An eigenvalue of a linearised flow is positive or negative, and there is no ambiguity about which microscopic details survive coarse-graining. In the learning case there is no flow whose eigenvalues one can inspect, and what gets preserved is implicit in a loss function and a dataset.

A neural network trained on configurations of the Ising model, the lattice the application accompanying this book simulates live, is given exactly the microscopic data that universality declares irrelevant: individual spin values, site by site, at some particular temperature on some particular lattice. If that information does not determine the macroscopic behaviour, and a machine trained on nothing else nevertheless recovers the transition, then either the machine is finding structure that coarse-graining discards without needing, or irrelevance is subtler than an eigenvalue calculation makes it look. What, then, does a machine learn when it learns from configurations whose details, by the best theory available, do not matter?
