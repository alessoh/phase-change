# Chapter 3: Disorder, Absolute Zero, and Emergence

In 1975 David Sherrington and Scott Kirkpatrick published a model built to be easy. Take an Ising system and couple every pair of spins, not merely neighbouring ones, through an independent Gaussian random interaction. Infinite range is the condition under which mean-field theory is supposed to be exact rather than approximate, so the answer should have been clean. Sherrington and Kirkpatrick solved the model by the replica trick under the simplest available assumption, that all replicas enter the solution symmetrically, and found both a spin-glass and a ferromagnetic phase. The same solution gave a negative entropy at zero temperature, $-k_B/2\pi$ per spin, about $-0.17$ in units of Boltzmann's constant.

Entropy counts configurations, and a count cannot fall below zero. The model was well posed and the technique standard, so the impossible number had to come from the assumed form of the solution rather than from any step of the algebra. It is derivable in half a page, and that derivation comes later in this chapter, once the rest of the argument is in place.

The premise it violated is the quiet content of the previous chapter. Lev Landau's prescription for a continuous transition is to identify an order parameter that vanishes in the symmetric phase, expand the free energy in powers of it, and read off the consequences; Kenneth Wilson's renormalization group later explained, through the fixed points of a flow in the space of coupling constants, why microscopically different systems share the resulting exponents. Someone has to hand you the order parameter before either construction starts, and Landau's rule, which says what a symmetry must do, does not say what field to write down when the answer is not a magnetisation or a density.

That first step has no theory behind it. It is a search over functions of a configuration for one whose average separates the phases, and it is a guess, historically made by a few people over a few decades. Stated that way it is the kind of problem a learned representation is built for, which is why these exceptions matter to the rest of this book more than the previous chapter's successes do. A search finds only what its function class contains, and learns only from configurations it is shown.

The four transitions below do not share a failure mode, which is the reason for putting them together. In two dimensions the free energy is intact and there is no local order parameter to be had. In percolation the order parameter is intact and there is no free energy. At a quantum critical point both survive and the critical point lies at a temperature no experiment reaches. In the spin glass the order parameter exists but is not a number, a vector or a tensor. Each break makes a different demand on anything that would automate the search.

## Where the symmetry cannot break

In 1966 Norman Mermin and Herbert Wagner proved that continuous symmetries cannot be spontaneously broken at any nonzero temperature in one- or two-dimensional systems with sufficiently short-range interactions, ruling out conventional long-range order in the two-dimensional Heisenberg and XY models. A broken continuous symmetry permits long-wavelength distortions whose energy cost vanishes as the wavevector goes to zero, so the mean-square phase fluctuation involves $\int d^dk \, / \, k^2$, which converges at small $k$ for $d > 2$ and diverges for $d \le 2$. In two dimensions the soft modes consume the order they are fluctuations of.

The theorem forbids what Landau's picture would otherwise predict, and forbids nothing else. Vadim Berezinskii identified what happens instead, describing the destruction of long-range order in one- and two-dimensional systems with a continuous symmetry group by means of topological excitations, in work whose Russian original is dated 1970 and whose English translation appeared in 1971. John Kosterlitz and David Thouless, at the University of Birmingham, defined topological order for two-dimensional systems that have no conventional long-range order, and described the transition as an unbinding of vortex pairs, bound and neutral below it, free above it and destroying the stiffness. That was 1973, and Kosterlitz supplied the renormalization-group treatment the following year.

The mechanism is a free-energy balance. Take a film of radius $L$ with phase field $\theta(\mathbf{r})$ and energy $\tfrac{1}{2}K\int d^2r \, (\nabla\theta)^2$, where $K$ is the stiffness. A vortex is a configuration in which $\theta$ winds once around a point, so $|\nabla\theta| = 1/r$ at distance $r$ from the core, and integrating from a core radius $a$ out to $L$ gives $E = \pi K \ln(L/a)$. The core can sit anywhere, which offers of order $(L/a)^2$ distinguishable positions, so its entropy is $S = 2 k_B \ln(L/a)$. Energy and entropy carry the same logarithm, which is what leaves the competition undecided:

$$F = E - TS = (\pi K - 2 k_B T)\ln(L/a).$$

The sign changes at $k_B T_{\mathrm{BKT}} = \pi K / 2$. Below that temperature $F$ grows without bound with the size of the film, so isolated vortices are suppressed and the survivors are neutral pairs, whose fields cancel beyond the pair separation. Above it a free vortex lowers the free energy by an amount that grows with the system, the pairs are pulled apart, and the phase field is scrambled at long distances. The balance also fixes a number no microscopic detail can move: at the transition the dimensionless stiffness is $K/k_B T_{\mathrm{BKT}} = 2/\pi$, whatever the film is made of. What results is a transition at which the free energy is non-analytic while every derivative of it stays continuous, and at which the correlation length diverges faster than $|t|^{-\nu}$ for any $\nu$. The exponent does not exist.

The deeper oddity is what is ordered. No local field has a thermal average that separates the phases, since Mermin and Wagner had shown that no such average survives. What separates them is whether the vortices are bound or free, and a vortex is a defect in the phase field rather than a value of it. Consider what that asks of a procedure searching a space of functions for one that discriminates. Restricted to linear functions of the spins, or to their low moments, it finds nothing and is right to report nothing. Yet the discriminating quantity is not remote from the data: the charge of a vortex is a sum of phase differences around one plaquette, about as local a measurement as a lattice permits. What is nonlocal is only the aggregation. The useful function is a sum over the lattice of a nonlinear function of a small neighbourhood, which is a claim about function classes rather than about physics. Landau's recipe fails here because it looks only among mean values of fields, and the object required is one order of composition away from anything a mean value can express.

The 2016 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for theoretical discoveries of topological phase transitions and topological phases of matter, one half to David J. Thouless of the University of Washington, Seattle, the other half jointly to F. Duncan M. Haldane of Princeton University and to Kosterlitz, of Brown University. Berezinskii had died in 1980 and was ineligible.

## A transition with nothing to minimise

Two dimensions removes the order parameter and leaves the thermodynamics standing. It is possible to lose the thermodynamics instead. In 1957 Simon Broadbent and John Hammersley founded percolation theory by separating a random medium from a deterministic fluid moving through it, and established a sharp critical probability $p_c$ above which an infinite connected cluster appears. In the bond version each edge of a lattice is open with probability $p$, independently of every other edge. There is no Hamiltonian, no energy, no temperature; nothing is minimised and nothing fluctuates thermally. What changes at $p_c$ is the connectivity of a random structure.

Almost all of the previous chapter's vocabulary survives the loss. The order parameter is the probability $P_\infty(p)$ that a site belongs to the infinite cluster, and it vanishes continuously as $(p - p_c)^\beta$. A connectivity length $\xi$, read off the decay of the probability that two sites lie in the same finite cluster, diverges as $|p - p_c|^{-\nu}$ on both sides. The threshold depends on everything microscopic, while $\beta$ and $\nu$ depend only on the dimension.

Both the threshold and an exponent can be had exactly on a tree, which plays the role mean-field theory plays for a magnet, because a tree has no loops and every branch is independent of every other. Let each site have $z$ neighbours and let $Q$ be the probability that a given branch fails to lead to an infinite cluster. Each of its $z - 1$ onward edges is either closed or open onto a branch that itself fails, so

$$Q = (1 - p + pQ)^{z-1}.$$

The value $Q = 1$ always solves this, and a second solution splits off from it when the derivative of the right-hand side at $Q = 1$ reaches unity, that is when $p(z-1) = 1$, so $p_c = 1/(z-1)$. Writing $Q = 1 - \epsilon$ just above threshold and expanding to second order, the linear terms cancel at $p_c$ and leave $\epsilon$ proportional to $p - p_c$. A site misses the infinite cluster only if all $z$ of its branches fail, so $P_\infty = 1 - (1 - p\epsilon)^{z} \simeq z p \epsilon$, also linear. On a tree $\beta = 1$ exactly, obtained without a partition function because there is none. The same exponents fix the geometry: a box of side $\xi$ holds of order $\xi^{d}|p-p_c|^{\beta}$ cluster sites, so with $|p - p_c| \sim \xi^{-1/\nu}$ the incipient cluster is a fractal of dimension $d_f = d - \beta/\nu$, spanning the system while occupying a vanishing fraction of it.

None of this came from a free energy. Criticality here is a property of the structure of a configuration space, and a partition function is one route to it rather than its definition. For a search over functions of a configuration this case is harder than the two-dimensional one, and harder in a specific way. The discriminating property is connectivity, and connectivity is not a function of any bounded window: two configurations can agree on every neighbourhood of radius $R$, for any fixed $R$, and differ in whether an open path crosses the sample. No amount of nonlinearity applied to a fixed receptive field repairs that; the field itself has to grow with the system. Two dimensions asks for composition, and percolation asks for range.

## A transition nobody can reach

Both of those cases lose a piece of the formalism. The third loses neither and loses the experiment instead. In 1976 John Hertz, at the James Franck Institute and the University of Chicago, built a quantum generalisation of the Landau-Ginzburg-Wilson functional by means of Hubbard-Stratonovich transformations, in which the order-parameter field depends on imaginary time as well as on space. Quantum statistical mechanics turns a system in $d$ spatial dimensions into a path integral over a slab whose thickness in imaginary time is the inverse temperature, and that thickness diverges as the temperature approaches zero. Time does not scale like space, so if lengths scale as $\xi$ then times scale as $\xi^z$, and the effective dimension becomes $d_{\text{eff}} = d + z$.

The dynamical exponent is not free. In a metal it is fixed by what the order-parameter fluctuation decays into: a spin fluctuation of wavevector $\mathbf{q}$ can decay by exciting a particle-hole pair across the Fermi surface, so its propagator acquires a damping term $|\omega|/\Gamma_q$ competing with the static $q^2$. For an antiferromagnet the ordering wavevector is finite, the particle-hole pairs available there have a nonvanishing density of states, $\Gamma_q$ tends to a constant, and $\omega \sim q^2$, so $z = 2$. For a clean ferromagnet the ordering wavevector is zero, the excitations available at small $q$ have energies of order $v_F q$, so $\Gamma_q \sim q$ and $\omega \sim q^3$, giving $z = 3$. Three dimensions therefore delivers $d_{\text{eff}} = 5$ for the itinerant antiferromagnet and $6$ for the itinerant ferromagnet, both above four, the upper critical dimension of the standard order-parameter theory and the value about which Wilson and Michael Fisher organised their expansion in $d = 4 - \epsilon$ dimensions. Mean-field exponents, wrong at every finite-temperature transition in three dimensions, come back at absolute zero, and they come back because the imaginary-time direction is worth two or three spatial dimensions rather than one.

Subir Sachdev's monograph gave the resulting field a settled vocabulary. A quantum phase transition occurs at zero temperature, driven not by heat but by a non-thermal control parameter such as pressure, magnetic field, or doping, with quantum rather than thermal fluctuations doing the work, and the critical point, though located at $T = 0$, governs a fan-shaped region of anomalous behaviour opening upward into the finite-temperature plane. The fan follows from the exponents already in play. A control parameter $g$ held a distance $|g - g_c|$ from its critical value gives $\xi \sim |g - g_c|^{-\nu}$ and an energy scale $\Delta \sim \xi^{-z}$, so the system behaves as though it sat at the critical point whenever $k_B T \gg |g - g_c|^{\nu z}$, and the crossover boundary is $T \sim |g - g_c|^{\nu z}$ on either side of $g_c$.

Andrew Millis took up the finite-temperature question in 1993, in a paper on the effect of a nonzero temperature on quantum critical points in itinerant fermion systems, and reported results differing from Hertz's in several cases. The difficulty is worth naming, since it is why the fan is harder than it looks. Above the upper critical dimension the quartic coupling flows to zero and is irrelevant in the renormalization-group sense, yet physical quantities depend on it in a way that does not vanish with it, so the free energy is not a simple scaling function of the remaining variables and the crossover scales cannot be obtained by counting dimensions. The pairing of the two papers is what the literature calls Hertz-Millis theory, or Hertz-Millis-Moriya theory in the review by Hilbert v. Löhneysen, Achim Rosch, Matthias Vojta and Peter Wölfle. There is an outer boundary too. Andrey Chubukov, Catherine Pépin and Jerome Rech showed in 2004 that for itinerant ferromagnets a universal negative, nonanalytic contribution to the static magnetic susceptibility destabilises the quantum critical point, so that the transition becomes first order or goes to incommensurate ordering instead. That is the $z = 3$ case, where the order-parameter theory predicts a continuous transition of a type that does not occur.

The obstruction here is not representational but evidential. The critical point sits at a temperature no experiment attains, so every measurement is taken inside the fan, and the object inferred is a point in a parameter space no apparatus visits. A search over functions of the data is asked to locate a singularity from samples none of which were drawn at it, with only the shape of a crossover boundary as signal. Expressiveness buys nothing against that. Two dimensions asks for composition, percolation asks for range, and this asks for extrapolation off the support of the data.

## The order parameter that had to be a function

Which returns the argument to Sherrington and Kirkpatrick, and to the negative number.

The physical setting was described in the same year by Samuel Edwards and Philip Anderson, at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, in the first theory of spin glasses. In dilute magnetic alloys the RKKY interaction oscillates in sign with distance, so couplings are ferromagnetic at some separations and antiferromagnetic at others, with no net order of either kind, and yet below a freezing temperature the spins lock into definite random directions. Edwards and Anderson proposed the order parameter that carries their names, $q = \overline{\langle S_i \rangle^2}$, the square of the time-averaged local magnetisation averaged over sites, and showed that it accounts for the cusp observed experimentally in the susceptibility. It does not measure what the spins point toward but whether they point anywhere at all, so the broken symmetry has no nameable direction.

The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model is the mean-field version of the same physics. Because the disorder is quenched, the quantity to be averaged over realisations of the couplings is $\log Z$ rather than $Z$, and the replica method reaches it through $\overline{\log Z} = \lim_{n \to 0} (\overline{Z^n} - 1)/n$. Coupling the copies through the disorder average leaves an overlap matrix $q_{ab}$ between replicas, and replica symmetry is the assumption that this matrix has a single off-diagonal value, $q_{ab} = q$ for all $a \ne b$, which hardly looks like an assumption, since the copies are identical by construction. With it, the free energy per spin for couplings of variance $J^2/N$ is

$$-\beta f = \frac{(\beta J)^2}{4}(1-q)^2 + \int \! Dz \, \ln 2\cosh\!\left(\beta J \sqrt{q}\, z\right),$$

where $Dz$ is the unit Gaussian measure and $q$ extremises the right-hand side. Now take $T$ to zero. The spins freeze, so $q \to 1$, and both terms must be expanded together because each contributes at the same order. Writing $a = \beta J \sqrt{q}$, the saddle-point condition gives $1 - q = \int Dz \, \mathrm{sech}^2(az) \simeq \sqrt{2/\pi}\,/a$, so the first term tends to the constant $1/2\pi$. In the second, $\ln 2\cosh(az) \to a|z|$ and $\int Dz\,|z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}$, but $a$ carries the factor $\sqrt{q} \simeq 1 - 1/(a\sqrt{2\pi})$, which subtracts a further constant $1/\pi$. The two do not cancel:

$$-\beta f \simeq \beta J \sqrt{2/\pi} - \frac{1}{2\pi}, \qquad f \simeq -J\sqrt{2/\pi} + \frac{k_B T}{2\pi}.$$

The free energy is linear in temperature with a positive slope, so $S = -\partial f/\partial T = -k_B/2\pi$. The whole of the pathology sits in that constant, and it does not come from the Gaussian integrals. It comes from the single number $q$.

In 1979 Giorgio Parisi showed that the mean-field spin glass requires not one order parameter but an infinite number of them, a full function $q(x)$ on the interval $[0,1]$, obtained by breaking the permutation symmetry among the replicas in a nested, hierarchical way, with the explicit scheme following in 1980. The construction is a sequence of block matrices. Divide the $n$ replicas into blocks of size $m_1$ and give $q_{ab}$ the value $q_1$ when $a$ and $b$ share a block and $q_0$ when they do not; then subdivide each block into blocks of size $m_2 < m_1$ and give the innermost pairs a third value, and so on, so that after $k$ steps the matrix is fixed by $n \ge m_1 \ge \cdots \ge m_k \ge 1$ together with $k+1$ overlaps. Continuation to $n \to 0$ reverses the inequalities, the block sizes become an increasing sequence in $[0,1]$, and as $k$ grows they fill the interval. The staircase becomes a function $q(x)$, and replica symmetry is the special case in which that function is constant.

The interpretation arrived four years after the function did. Take two configurations drawn independently from the same equilibrium distribution and define their overlap,

$$q_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i S_i^\alpha S_i^\beta,$$

a number measuring how much of the frozen pattern the two share; in an ordinary ferromagnet it takes one value up to a sign, there being only two states to draw from. In 1983 Parisi identified $q(x)$ as the inverse of the cumulative distribution of that overlap, so a $q(x)$ spread over an interval means many pure states at many different distances from one another, and the hierarchy of states is ultrametric: for any three, the two largest of the three mutual distances are equal, so the states sit at the leaves of a tree rather than at points of an ordinary space. Landau's rule derives the space in which the order parameter lives from the symmetry of the Hamiltonian, and here the symmetry that breaks is not a symmetry of the spins at all. It is a permutation symmetry among replicas, a device introduced to handle an average over disorder.

The consequence for any automated search is sharper than in the other three cases, and not a matter of degree. A function class can be enlarged and a receptive field can be grown, and even the extrapolation problem at a quantum critical point is a question of how much can be inferred from samples of the wrong kind. But $q(x)$ is not a function of a configuration. It is built from the overlap of two configurations drawn independently from the same distribution, which makes it a property of the measure rather than of any sample from it. A procedure that assigns numbers to single configurations, however expressive, cannot represent this object at all, because the object is not of that type. It is not that the search would fail. It would be looking in a space that does not contain the answer, and would report, correctly and uselessly, that the system has no order.

## What was contested, and what was proved

The proposal was disputed for a long time, and about physics rather than arithmetic. William McMillan set out a real-space scaling theory of Ising spin glasses in 1984 in which the ordered phase is characterised by a stiffness exponent governing the free-energy cost of large-scale domain walls. Daniel Fisher, who is not the Michael Fisher of the epsilon expansion, and David Huse built the droplet picture on that line, in a short paper in 1986 and at length in 1988, arguing from a scaling ansatz for low-lying droplet excitations to a spin-glass phase with only two pure states related by a global spin flip, a trivial overlap distribution, no ultrametricity, and no de Almeida-Thouless transition. That last item is the one an experiment can most nearly reach, since replica symmetry breaking predicts a de Almeida-Thouless line in a field, meaning the many-state structure survives an applied magnetic field, while the droplet picture holds that a field destroys the spin-glass phase outright. Two back-to-back letters in 1987, one by Huse and Fisher in that order and one by Fisher and Huse in the other, attacked the many-states claim directly. Charles Newman and Daniel Stein made the dispute three-cornered in 1996, offering proofs that the main features of the Parisi solution, carried over to short-range models, hold in no dimension at any temperature, and Enzo Marinari, Parisi, Federico Ricci-Tersenghi, Juan Ruiz-Lorenzo and Francesco Zuliani answered at length in 2000 with numerical evidence for replica symmetry breaking in finite dimensions.

For the mean-field model the question was closed by mathematicians. Francesco Guerra proved, by an interpolation argument, that the Parisi expression is a rigorous upper bound, uniform in system size, on $N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_N$; equivalently, since Guerra defines $-\beta f_N = N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_N$, the Parisi value is a rigorous lower bound on the quenched free energy per site. Michel Talagrand supplied the matching bound, announcing it in a four-page note in 2003 and publishing the full proof of the Parisi formula in 2006. Dmitry Panchenko closed the remaining gap in 2013 by proving the ultrametricity conjecture, so the formula had been a theorem for seven years before the hierarchical organisation of states became one.

The 2021 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for groundbreaking contributions to our understanding of complex physical systems, divided with one half jointly to Syukuro Manabe and Klaus Hasselmann for the physical modelling of Earth's climate, and the other half to Parisi, of Sapienza University of Rome, for the discovery of the interplay of disorder and fluctuations in physical systems from atomic to planetary scales. That reach is not ceremonial language. The replica method and the rugged-landscape picture are the direct ancestors of the material in Part Six of this book.

## More is different, and harder to anticipate

Philip Anderson, three years before the Edwards-Anderson paper and while at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Murray Hill, argued that the reductionist hypothesis does not imply a constructionist one. Knowing the microscopic laws does not entail being able to build the behaviour of a large assembly upward from them, because at each level of complexity new properties appear, and the science of one level is not simply applied science of the level below. The four cases here sharpen that. The standard reading is that new laws appear at new scales; the stronger reading, which the spin glass forces, is that the new laws may require mathematical objects of a kind not present anywhere in the description of the level below, and that no familiarity with the microscopic rules tells you what kind of object to look for. The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Hamiltonian is a line of undergraduate notation, and its solution is a function on the unit interval encoding an ultrametric tree.

Read as a list of requirements, the four cases specify what any procedure would need in order to find order parameters without being told what to look for. It must compose local nonlinearities, since the useful feature in two dimensions is a sum of them and no mean value expresses it. It must let its window grow with the system, since connectivity is decided by no bounded neighbourhood. It must infer a singularity from data drawn near it and never at it. And it must be prepared for the answer to be a property of the measure rather than of any configuration in it, because at least once the answer has been exactly that. The first three are engineering. The fourth is not, because a search cannot enlarge the type of its own output.

Each of the order parameters here was found by someone who happened to think of the right kind of object, after years in which competent people had looked at the same system and thought of nothing. There is no procedure for generating candidates, and no way to tell, from the microscopic model alone, whether a system resists description because it has no order or because its order lives in a space nobody has yet proposed. Somewhere among the systems now filed as disordered, and in the far larger set being sifted by methods that search function classes nobody has characterised, the second possibility is presumably realised. The question is how anyone would ever know.
