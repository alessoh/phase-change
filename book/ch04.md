# Chapter 4. The Barrier

Cool a liquid metal below its melting point and, for a long while, nothing happens. The rate theory that David Turnbull and John Crocker Fisher published in 1949 was followed by experiments in which Turnbull held liquid metals near eight tenths of their melting temperature without their crystallizing. Throughout, the crystal is the stable phase and the free energy difference in its favour is large and growing. The liquid stays liquid.

Equilibrium thermodynamics tells you which phase a system should be in. It does not tell you when the system will get there, and for a first-order transition those two statements can be separated by geological time. A treatment of machine learning applied to phase transitions that skips the kinetics will locate coexistence lines beautifully and say nothing about whether the transition is observable at all.

## Why a barrier exists at all

A first-order transition is one in which the first derivative of the thermodynamic potential is discontinuous. Entropy and volume jump, and the transition carries latent heat. Paul Ehrenfest proposed that classification by the order of the lowest discontinuous derivative in 1933, in a paper Tilman Sauer translated with historical commentary in 2017. The higher-order categories do not describe real continuous transitions, which show power-law divergences instead of finite jumps, so modern usage is first-order versus continuous. A jump in density or entropy means the two phases are genuinely different objects, and to get from liquid to crystal the system must build a piece of crystal inside the liquid, which has a boundary.

The cost of that boundary is the whole story. Its ancestry is the memoir of Josiah Willard Gibbs on the equilibrium of heterogeneous substances, abstracted in the American Journal of Science in 1878, which supplies the surface free energy of an interface and the reversible work of forming a new phase inside an existing one. Gibbs supplied the accounting; the twentieth century added the observation that it has a maximum in it.

Consider a spherical embryo of the stable phase, of radius $R$, inside the metastable parent, the two terms Max Volmer and Alfred Weber set against each other in 1926. The interior is cheaper by an amount proportional to its volume; the boundary costs an amount proportional to its area. Write $\Delta g_v$ for the free energy difference per unit volume, negative when the new phase is stable, and $\gamma$ for the interfacial free energy per unit area:

$$\Delta G(R) = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}\,\Delta g_v + 4\pi R^{2}\gamma .$$

For small $R$ the quadratic term dominates and $\Delta G$ rises; for large $R$ the cubic term wins and $\Delta G$ falls without limit. In between sits a maximum, at a critical radius

$$R^{*} = \frac{2\gamma}{|\Delta g_v|}, \qquad \Delta G^{*} = \frac{16\pi\gamma^{3}}{3\,\Delta g_v^{2}} .$$

An embryo smaller than $R^{*}$ lowers its free energy by dissolving, one larger by growing. The transition therefore begins not when the new phase becomes stable but when a fluctuation assembles a region larger than $R^{*}$, whose probability carries the Boltzmann factor $\exp(-\Delta G^{*}/k_B T)$.

Now write the driving force in terms of the undercooling. Near coexistence the enthalpy and entropy differences between the phases are each nearly constant, and their combination $\Delta h_v - T\Delta s_v$ vanishes at $T_m$, which is what makes $T_m$ the melting point. Write $L_v$ for the latent heat of fusion per unit volume and $\Delta T = T_m - T$, and hold $\gamma$ fixed, an assumption of convenience the end of the chapter returns to:

$$|\Delta g_v| \approx \frac{L_v \Delta T}{T_m}, \qquad \Delta G^{*} \approx \frac{16\pi\gamma^{3}T_m^{2}}{3L_v^{2}\,\Delta T^{2}} .$$

The barrier grows as the inverse square of the undercooling and is infinite at the melting point. A first-order transition must be pushed past its own equilibrium point before anything can happen, which makes metastability the generic condition.

## From a barrier to a rate

The equilibrium argument gives a population, not a flux. Richard Becker and Werner Döring supplied the kinetics in 1935 by treating the clusters of each size as a birth-and-death process, gaining and losing monomers at rates set by attachment and detachment. The steady-state flux through cluster-size space is the nucleation rate:

$$J = \rho\, Z\, f^{*}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G^{*}}{k_B T}\right),$$

where $\rho$ is the number density of sites at which a nucleus could form, so that $J$ is a rate per unit volume, $f^{*}$ is the rate at which single atoms attach to a critical nucleus, and $Z$ is the Zeldovich correction that the steady-state solution produces and the equilibrium argument cannot. With $\Delta G(n)$ the free energy of a cluster of $n$ atoms and $n^{*}$ the critical size, $Z$ is the square root of the barrier curvature over $2\pi k_BT$. The top of the barrier is flat: sizes within $k_BT$ of the maximum span a range of order $1/Z$ in $n$, and a cluster crossing that plateau diffuses, so most clusters reaching the top fall back before committing. The curvature is not free. The surface term at the critical size equals $3\Delta G^{*}$, so differentiating twice gives $|\partial^{2}\Delta G/\partial n^{2}| = 2\Delta G^{*}/3n^{*2}$ and

$$Z = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi k_B T}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\Delta G}{\partial n^{2}}\right|_{n^{*}}} = \sqrt{\frac{\Delta G^{*}}{3\pi k_B T\, n^{*2}}},$$

which for a barrier of $60\,k_BT$ and a nucleus of a hundred atoms is $2.5\times10^{-2}$. Attachment happens at the surface, so $f^{*}$ is the per-atom rate times the number of atoms touching the nucleus, of order $n^{*2/3}$, about twenty here.

In a melt an atom joining a crystalline nucleus must detach from the liquid and cross the interface, and that crossing has its own activation free energy $\Delta G_a$. Turnbull and Fisher supplied that term in 1949, giving classical nucleation theory the form used for crystallization from melts and solutions. Writing $\nu$ for the attempt frequency of a surface atom,

$$J = \rho\, Z\, n^{*2/3}\nu\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G_a}{k_B T}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{16\pi\gamma^{3}T_m^{2}}{3L_v^{2}\,k_B T\,\Delta T^{2}}\right).$$

Taking the logarithm makes the competition explicit. The thermodynamic exponent is enormous just below $T_m$ and decays roughly as $\Delta T^{-2}$ as the undercooling deepens. The transport exponent moves the other way: $\Delta G_a$ is the activation free energy for motion in the liquid, and it grows as $k_BT$ shrinks. Their sum has a single minimum, so $J$ has a single maximum. Above it the barrier controls the rate; below it, transport does, and a system there is thermodynamically desperate to crystallize and kinetically unable to.

Nothing here requires the nucleus to form in the bulk. A nucleus on a foreign surface replaces liquid-substrate interface with crystal-substrate interface, and the geometry of the resulting spherical cap is fixed by the contact angle $\theta$ at which the three interfacial tensions balance. The critical radius is unchanged, and the barrier changes by a geometric factor:

$$\Delta G^{*}_{\mathrm{het}} = \Delta G^{*} f(\theta), \qquad f(\theta) = \frac{(2+\cos\theta)(1-\cos\theta)^{2}}{4} .$$

That factor is the volume of the cap divided by the volume of the whole sphere of radius $R^{*}$: the fraction of the critical fluctuation the system still has to assemble for itself. It falls from one, for a surface the crystal will not wet, to zero as the crystal wets it completely, and at $\theta = 60^{\circ}$ it is $0.156$. Because the rate is exponential in the barrier, that is not a sixth of the difficulty, and it is why deep supercooling says as much about what has been kept out of a sample as about the liquid in it.

## The first phase to appear is often the wrong one

Because the barrier scales as $\gamma^{3}/\Delta g_v^{2}$, the phase that nucleates fastest need not be the most stable. A metastable polymorph presenting a low-energy interface against the parent liquid can have the smaller barrier even when its bulk driving force is weaker, because the cube beats the square. Wilhelm Ostwald recognized the pattern in 1897 and stated it as the rule of stages: a supercooled system tends first to form the least stable phase whose free energy lies nearest the parent, then to step down toward stability. The rule is kinetic, not thermodynamic, and it still organizes polymorph selection.

A simulation that finds the correct ground state can therefore predict the wrong outcome. The ab initio phase diagram of gallium, computed in 2020 by Haiyang Niu, Luigi Bonati, Pablo Piaggi and Michele Parrinello with a neural network potential trained on density functional data, reproduces the equilibria among liquid gallium, alpha-Ga, beta-Ga and Ga-II, and finds the metastable beta phase kinetically favoured over the thermodynamically stable alpha. On the timescale of an experiment the kinetics wins.

## The limit where there is no barrier

Everything above assumes the parent phase is metastable: locally stable against small fluctuations, globally unstable, separated from the stable phase by a barrier. That assumption fails inside the spinodal. John Werner Cahn and John Edward Hilliard abandoned the sharp interface in 1958 and wrote the free energy of a nonuniform system as a functional of a smoothly varying composition field,

$$F = N_V \int_V \left[ f_0(c) + \kappa\,(\nabla c)^{2} \right] dV,$$

with $f_0$ the homogeneous free energy per molecule and $\kappa$ the gradient-energy coefficient. The square-gradient term generates an interfacial energy instead of assuming one, which is what makes it the starting point for diffuse-interface and phase-field modelling. Part III of the series, in 1959, derived the critical nucleus as a saddle point of that functional and showed that the classical sharp-interface nucleus fails near the spinodal.

Cahn took the argument to its conclusion in 1961. Composition is conserved, so it obeys $\partial_t c = M\nabla^{2}(\delta F/\delta c)$ with $M$ a mobility, and to linear order in a perturbation $\delta c \propto \exp(i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r} + \omega t)$,

$$\omega(q) = -M q^{2}\left( f_0'' + 2\kappa q^{2} \right).$$

Where $f_0'' > 0$ every mode decays. Inside the spinodal, where $f_0'' < 0$, modes with $q^{2} < |f_0''|/2\kappa$ grow, fastest at $q_{\max}^{2} = |f_0''|/4\kappa$. There is no critical nucleus and no barrier. The gradient term suppresses short wavelengths, the negative curvature of $f_0$ amplifies long ones, and the wavelength between them sets the scale of the microstructure.

Two cautions attach to that boundary. Cahn showed that when the molar volume depends on composition, elastic energy shifts the true limit of metastability away from the chemical spinodal, so the line is model-dependent. The second is internal: the linear analysis treats $f_0(c)$ as a well-defined free energy for a state that cannot be prepared or held. Approached from the metastable side, the saddle point of the same functional replaces the compact classical nucleus by a diffuse object whose barrier falls continuously toward the zero found beyond the line. Nucleation and spinodal decomposition are two limits of one calculation, not two mechanisms with a line between them, and between the binodal and the spinodal the barrier is finite and the waiting problem is real.

## When the system stops arriving

Push the undercooling past the maximum of the rate curve and the theory predicts its own failure. $\Delta G^{*}$ keeps falling, so crystallization becomes ever more favourable; $\Delta G_a/k_BT$ keeps rising, so it becomes ever slower. The liquid thickens instead of freezing. Its relaxation time climbs by many orders of magnitude, exceeds the time available for the measurement, and the system falls out of equilibrium. What is left is a glass, and the transport factor that entered nucleation theory as a correction to a prefactor has become the entire problem.

What lies underneath the arrest is contested, and the dispute is about that exponent. Walter Kauzmann observed in 1948 that the excess entropy of a supercooled liquid over its crystal, extrapolated below the temperature at which the glass actually forms, would reach zero at a finite temperature and then go negative. Nothing forbids an amorphous state of lower entropy than a crystal, so the extrapolation is not a contradiction but an invitation to believe that something intervenes first, and the temperature $T_K$ at which the excess entropy would vanish is the standard anchor for an ideal glass transition hidden below the arrest that preempts it.

Gerald Adam and Julian Gibbs supplied a mechanism in 1965 that turns the transport exponent into a thermodynamic quantity. Separate the entropy of a liquid into a vibrational part, counting motion within one amorphous packing, and a configurational part $s_c$ per particle, counting the packings available. Suppose relaxation proceeds by the cooperative rearrangement of a compact region, which can move only if it has two configurations of its own, so that its configurational entropy $z s_c$ must reach about $k_B\ln 2$. The smallest such region, $z^{*} \simeq k_B\ln 2 / s_c$, grows as the liquid runs out of configurations. If the cost of moving it is proportional to the number of particles in it, $\Delta G_a = z^{*}\Delta\mu$, then

$$\tau = \tau_0 \exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta G_a}{k_B T}\right) = \tau_0 \exp\!\left(\frac{A}{T S_c}\right),$$

with $S_c$ the configurational entropy per mole and $A$ a constant. That exponent is the same $\Delta G_a/k_BT$ that sat in the transport factor of the nucleation rate. Adam and Gibbs did not add a term to the theory of the previous section; they gave its activation free energy a thermodynamic argument. Now let the configurational entropy vanish at $T_K$ in the combination $T S_c \simeq \Delta c_p (T - T_K)$, which is what an excess specific heat falling as $1/T$ produces on integration:

$$\tau = \tau_0 \exp\!\left(\frac{A}{\Delta c_p\,(T - T_K)}\right).$$

That is the VFT form used to fit the relaxation of glass-forming liquids, and it is close to the empirical WLF law as well. A divergence fitted to viscosity data now lands on a temperature extracted from calorimetry. That coincidence is the appeal of the entropy-crisis picture and close to the whole of its evidence, since the divergence is never observed and the extrapolation never reached.

A rival tradition puts the arrest in the dynamics alone. Mode-coupling theory, developed by Ulf Bengtzelius, Wolfgang Götze and Arne Sjölander in 1984, starts from the normalized correlation $\phi_q(t)$ of density fluctuations at wavevector $q$, whose exact equation of motion,

$$\ddot\phi_q(t) + \Omega_q^{2}\phi_q(t) + \int_0^{t} m_q(t-t')\,\dot\phi_q(t')\,dt' = 0,$$

is not a theory until the memory kernel $m_q$ is specified. The closure keeps the slowest object the kernel can be built from, a pair of density modes, so that $m_q(t) \approx \sum_{k} V(q;k)\,\phi_k(t)\,\phi_{|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}|}(t)$ with the vertices $V$ computed from the static structure factor alone. The equation now feeds on its own solution. In the long-time limit, with $f_q = \phi_q(\infty)$, it collapses to an algebraic self-consistency of the form $f_q/(1-f_q) = m_q[f]$. At weak coupling the only solution is $f_q = 0$: correlations decay and the liquid flows. Sharpen the structure factor by compressing or cooling and a second solution appears discontinuously at finite $f_q$, correlations never decay, and the liquid is arrested.

Nothing in that argument differentiated a free energy. The bifurcation is a property of the closure: it is where a quadratic self-consistency acquires a second root, and it would happen the same way for any equation of that shape. That is why it carries no thermodynamic singularity, and why the sharp transition it predicts, which experiment does not find, is now read as a crossover. For hard spheres the calculation puts the bifurcation near a packing fraction of 0.516, a figure to be carried with the caution that the object it locates is not sharp.

## The athermal version, and why it is in this chapter

Chapter 20 will argue that neural networks jam. An analogy of that kind can only be judged by a reader who knows what the physical transition asserts and what it leaves open, so the physical version belongs here, beside the arrest it generalizes.

Andrea Liu and Sidney Nagel proposed in 1998 that granular materials, foams, emulsions and supercooled liquids all sit on one phase diagram, with axes of temperature, applied load and inverse density, so that thermal glass formation and granular jamming are a single transition approached along different directions. Temperature, the only axis the previous section used, stops being privileged. Corey O'Hern, Leonardo Silbert, Liu and Nagel located the athermal corner in 2003. Frictionless spheres with finite-range repulsion, at zero temperature and zero stress, jam at a threshold packing fraction that varies between configurations, with a distribution that narrows as the system grows and converges to random close packing. That limiting point, point J, behaves like a critical point, with power-law scaling of contact number, pressure and elastic moduli, and its exponents do not depend on dimension, which is the usual signature of criticality, but do depend on the interparticle potential, which is not.

Whether any of the arrest rests on a thermodynamic transition is open. Reviewing the field in 2011, Ludovic Berthier and Giulio Biroli set mode-coupling theory, the entropy-crisis picture, dynamic facilitation and kinetically constrained models side by side and adjudicated among none of them. Taking stock in 2023, Biroli and Jean-Philippe Bouchaud found that the theory built on the entropy crisis reproduces the salient experimental facts of supercooled liquids very well and that its thermodynamic side has gained support, while direct experimental validation remains elusive. The activation free energy that nucleation theory carried as a correction is, in the arrest, the entire subject, and nobody agrees what quantity it is a function of.

## How long the wait actually is

Put numbers in. Take $\Delta G^{*} = 60\,k_BT$, a moderate barrier at moderate undercooling, and a critical nucleus of about a hundred atoms. The transport exponent is small there, so set $\exp(-\Delta G_a/k_BT)$ to one. The attempt frequency is not a fitted quantity: it is a lattice vibration, of order $k_B\Theta_D/h$ with $\Theta_D$ a Debye temperature of a few hundred kelvin, which gives $\nu \sim 10^{13}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ to within a factor of a few. About twenty atoms sit on the nucleus surface, so $f^{*} \approx 2\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and the curvature formula gives $Z = 2.5\times10^{-2}$. The prefactor is $Zf^{*} \approx 5\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

That is a rate for one site, meaning $J/\rho$ and not $J$. A site is one atom's worth of volume, since $\rho$ in the Becker-Döring expression is conventionally the atomic number density, so a cell of $10^{5}$ atoms carries $10^{5}$ sites. That overcounts, since neighbouring atoms cannot host independent nuclei, but by at most the hundred atoms in a nucleus: two orders of magnitude, in a problem whose answer is about to move by twenty.

The rate per site is $5\times10^{12}e^{-60} \approx 4\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so the wait per site is $2\times10^{13}$ seconds, about seven hundred thousand years. Give the cell its $10^{5}$ sites and the wait falls to $2\times10^{8}$ seconds, roughly seven years of simulated time, against the microseconds that molecular dynamics with a machine-learned potential reaches. That is fourteen orders of magnitude, and the prefactor did not help: the Zeldovich factor costs forty, the surface counting returns twenty, and the honest prefactor is half the bare vibrational frequency, so the wait is twice what $\nu^{-1}e^{60}$ would suggest.

Notice how little that depends on the number stipulated. At $\Delta G^{*} = 40\,k_BT$ the same formulas give $Z = 2.1\times10^{-2}$, a prefactor of $4\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and a wait for the whole cell of about half a second, still six orders of magnitude beyond a microsecond run. At $90\,k_BT$ they give $Z = 3.1\times10^{-2}$, $6\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $2\times10^{21}$ seconds, a number with no physical referent. The prefactor moves by half across that range; the answer moves by twenty-two orders of magnitude. The barrier is the only quantity that matters, which is why the rest of this book cares how it is estimated.

Cooling further does not help: past the maximum of the rate curve the simulation waits on transport instead. Put a substrate in the same cell with a contact angle of sixty degrees and the barrier becomes $9.4\,k_BT$, the wait per site drops to nanoseconds, and the cell freezes before the first frame is written. Everything in the estimate is exponential, including the geometry.

## Sampling what never happens

This is why the tooling looks the way it does. A history-dependent bias potential can be made to fill the free-energy minima of selected collective variables progressively, so that a trajectory escapes a metastable state and the surface is reconstructed afterwards: that is metadynamics, introduced by Alessandro Laio and Michele Parrinello in 2002. A well-tempered variant followed in 2008 from Alessandro Barducci, Giovanni Bussi and Parrinello, rescaling the deposited bias so that it converges to a tunable fraction of the free energy instead of diffusing indefinitely. Rates come by other means again. Pablo Piaggi, Jack Weis, Athanassios Panagiotopoulos, Pablo Debenedetti and Roberto Car computed homogeneous ice nucleation rates in 2022, at atmospheric pressure and across a broad range of supercoolings, by seeding: inserting a nucleus instead of awaiting one, with a model trained on density functional energies and forces.

Each of these methods makes the rare configuration common, then corrects for the change afterwards, and training data for a machine-learned potential is made the same way. Luigi Bonati and Parrinello, in 2018, trained a deep neural network potential on data seeded by metadynamics sampling with a classical potential, then drove silicon crystallization with a collective variable derived from the Debye structure factor and recovered the free-energy surface at density functional accuracy. Piaggi and colleagues put the nucleus in by hand. Both training sets therefore contain configurations from the barrier region, and the objection that a network has only ever seen equilibrium liquid and equilibrium crystal misses its target.

The sharper problem is that a biased sampling scheme is defined by a collective variable, and a collective variable is a hypothesis about the reaction coordinate. The potential learns the part of the barrier region that the hypothesis chose to visit, any rate it reports is conditioned on that choice, and nothing in the output records the conditioning. The conditioning is expensive: since $\Delta G^{*}$ goes as $\gamma^{3}$, a three per cent error in the interfacial free energy is a nine per cent error in the barrier, which at $60\,k_BT$ is $5.4\,k_BT$ and a factor of two hundred in the rate.

And this chapter has been assuming an answer for $\gamma$ throughout. Every formula above gave a hundred-atom cluster the interfacial free energy of a flat boundary between bulk phases, constant in temperature and independent of curvature. That assumption is what turned a functional of the composition profile into a single number, and it is the kind of approximation a first-principles potential was brought in to remove. A calculation that inherits it has moved it into the collective variable.

For crystallization the hypothesis is at least plausible, since the product phase has an order the parent lacks. For the arrest of a supercooled liquid it is worse: the review of Liu and Nagel in 2010 describes the marginally jammed solid as rigid while structurally disordered on both sides of the transition, so the coordinate the system travels along leaves no trace in the structure. A bias potential must be applied along some variable, and here nobody agrees which.

So the critical nucleus is, by construction, the configuration the system visits least, and every picture of it in every training set was put there by a scheme that had already decided what it should look like. Whether a barrier learned that way is a measurement of the barrier or an echo of the assumption used to reach it is not a question that can be put to the model.
